## 1. Purpose

Drinking, cooking, and washing water for everyone on site at capacity, in
every season and through power outages, with a reserve in the shelter.
Spring, gravity, sand, and stone — built by local trades and repairable by
the people who use it.

**Four rules.** Gravity before pumps: no step that supplies people
depends on electricity. Two waters kept apart: spring water for people,
roof water for plants, sharing no pipe. Storage covers the gap: three
days of demand held, so a dry spell, a frozen line, or a repair does not
stop a session. Treatment assumes contamination will happen.

**Connections.** Depends on: the spring measurement; the enquiries on
the protection zone and a fire-fighting reserve; the energy module for
the UV unit on the essential circuit. Feeds: the greywater, reed bed,
and pond module with all used water; the shelter with its reserve; the
rainwater module takes the non-drinking uses when the spring falls
between thresholds.

**Patterns.** Grows from: not yet named. How homesteads here stand to
their spring, and whether this spring is held in any way that decides
how it may be touched, are questions for the elders before the spring
box is built. Feeds: not yet named. Resonates with: 207 Good Materials
— stone from the site or the valley, built and repaired by local
trades; 83 Master and Apprentices — paid hands learning the stonework
beside the mason; 104 Site Repair — the cistern and the catchment
fence on the poorer ground. In tension with: 64 Pools and Streams — a
buried cistern and a piped main take water out of sight; the spring
and its catchment are where it can stay visible. 25 Access to Water —
the catchment fence keeps people back from water the pattern would
open to them.

## 3. Sizing method

**What you must measure yourself before using this module:**

- **Spring yield in the driest month** — late August, or a hard February
  with frozen ground. A bucket of known volume and a stopwatch, three
  readings, keep the lowest. Record the date and the weather of the
  preceding weeks.
- **Height difference** between spring, cistern site, and highest tap,
  and the real pipe lengths — from a site walk.
- **Your group size at capacity** and your sanitation type.

**Choose a daily figure per person.**

| Level | L per person per day |
|---|---|
| Survival minimum, humanitarian standard | 15 |
| Basic health and hygiene, WHO threshold | 50 |
| Rustic residential, dry sanitation | 60–80 |
| Institutional, flush toilets and showers | 100–150 |
| European household average | 130–145 |

The household figure is not a design number for a site with dry
sanitation and communal washing. The European Environment Agency puts
direct household use at roughly 130 to 145 L per person per day, a
quarter to a third of it toilet flushing.

**Our example:** water_per_person, 75 L a day, everyone on site alike,
kitchen included; capacity, 34 people; storage_days, 3. Values from
the site parameters file, held in the workshop.

| Quantity | Calculation | Our example |
|---|---|---|
| Daily demand | people × L per day | 34 × 75 = 2,550 L |
| Storage, three days | 3 × daily demand | 7,650 L, built as 8 m³ |
| Spring yield, bare minimum | daily demand ÷ 1,440 min | 1.8 L/min |
| Spring yield, design | minimum × 2, rounded up | 4 L/min |
| Shelter reserve, 48 h | people × 3 L × 2 days | 204 L |
| Sand filter area | daily volume ÷ rate | 0.5–1.1 m² |
| Pressure at the tap | about 1 bar per 10 m of height | 1–2 bar |

The sand filter figure takes 2.55 m³ a day at a filtration rate of 0.1
to 0.2 m per hour. The 1 m² costed in Section 6 is enough at the faster
rate and short at the slower one: 1.1 m² is needed to filter at 0.1 m per
hour. The 8 m³ cistern holds 3.1 days at 34 people, so its size and cost
stand.

**Read the spring against the demand.**

- 4 L/min or more: the spring carries the site alone.
- Between 1.8 and 4 L/min: it carries the people only if rainwater takes
  every non-drinking use it can.
- Below 1.8 L/min: a second source is needed before anything is built.

| Fill, flush, refill 2–3× | household | [from L2] | lime in the water |
| Laboratory test | laboratory | [from L2] | no record for inspection |

**Details that decide whether it works.** The spring box: a collection
chamber, a screened overflow, a lockable lid, a clean-out, the ground
around it sealed with clay and sloped so rain runs away from the box.
The cistern: inlet through a settling box; outlet 10 to 15 cm above the
floor; a washout at the lowest point; an overflow with a flap valve; a
screened vent; one lockable manhole per chamber; walls meeting the floor
in a rounded cove so the render does not crack at the corners.

**Structure.** A buried cistern carries earth pressure when empty and
water pressure when full. A local builder or engineer confirms the
walls against the soil before building.

**Ukraine, unchecked.** The source states that Ukrainian rules set
sanitary protection zones around drinking-water sources, typically 30
to 50 m for the first belt around groundwater, without naming the
instrument. The zone for a given source is confirmed with the district
sanitary service before the fence line is set. Whether the State
Emergency Service requires a separate fire-fighting water reserve is
open, and could exceed this system. A children's establishment will be
asked for water test results by the sanitary service.

## 11. Adaptation notes

- **A drier valley:** measure first; below 4 L/min, move every
  non-drinking use to rainwater; below 1.8 L/min, a second source before
  anything is built.
- **A colder valley:** [set] — the design already assumes at least 1 m
  of earth cover, below the frost line.
- **A smaller group:** recalculate with Section 3; storage stays at three
  days of demand.
- **A larger group:** recalculate with Section 3; the sand filter scales
  with daily volume.
- **Flush toilets:** use the institutional figure, 100 to 150 L per
  person per day.
