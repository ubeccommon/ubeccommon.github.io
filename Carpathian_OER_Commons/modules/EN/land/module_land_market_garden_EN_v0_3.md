*Maturity: **L0**, a marked draft — sized and designed, not yet fully
costed, and published so that it can be read and changed in the open.
The establishment compost, the seed, the tarps, the hoops, the pond,
and its pump are not priced, and nothing has been grown. Read
every figure below at this level and no higher. The module moves to L1
when every line in Section 6 carries a dated cost and its basis.*

*Governed by Section 2 of the compendium. No child appears in this
module in any form. The site is not named; site plans are schematic;
photographs show structures and systems only. Design loads are site
capacity, used for sizing only. No market, town, buyer, route, or day
of distribution is named, and no list of those who eat from the garden
enters the repository.*

*"Market garden" names the method — intensive vegetable growing on
permanent beds — and not a sale. The garden serves the hamlet's table
first; what leaves the hamlet, once the hamlet is fed, goes through the
growers' and eaters' association, a module of its own in Settlement.*

## 1. Purpose

Fresh vegetables for the hamlet's kitchen and table at capacity, grown
on permanent beds by a paid grower, on ground fed from the garden's own
nutrient stream. What the kitchen cannot use fresh goes to the food
storage module; what the hamlet does not need, once it is fed, goes to
the association.

**Five rules.** The hamlet eats first: the region receives only a
measured surplus. Two nutrient streams kept apart: the toilet stream
feeds trees; only the garden's own stream reaches the beds. The grower
is paid: the garden's work is a cost line, never a contribution, and
toloka is not the name for work the garden depends on. Ecologically
oriented, not certified: the garden describes its practice as
ecologically oriented and seeks no certification for now; its produce is
not labelled organic, bio, or eco, words Ukrainian law keeps for
certified produce. Spring water is for people: the garden is never
irrigated from the spring, but from a small pond the stream refills.

**Connections.** Depends on: the Phase 1 walk of the ground, a soil
analysis, and the season measured or taken from the nearest station; the
compost and site nutrient cycle module for the garden's own stream, kept
apart from the toilet stream, and the biochar module for char charged in
that stream; a small pond, always refilled by the stream, as its
irrigation source, decided 2026-09-28 — the rainwater and nursery
irrigation module keeps the nursery; the energy module for the pump on
the essential or seasonal circuit; the water module for washing produce
in spring water; the legal form decision, since the garden is a
registered capacity of the operator that runs the kitchen. Feeds: the
kitchen and dining module; the food storage module, whose cold room the
garden needs from the first harvest that is not eaten the same day; the
growers' and eaters' association, with its measured surplus only. The
terra preta module does not feed the beds; its product goes to trees, as
the sanitation working paper holds.

**Patterns.** Grows from: not yet named. Where vegetables were grown in
this valley relative to the house, the hayfield, and the slope; how that
ground was fed; and who kept the seed, are questions for the elders
before the first bed is dug. Feeds: not yet named. Resonates with: 177
Vegetable Garden — ground set aside for vegetables, sunny, fenced, with
its tool store beside it; 169 Terraced Slope — permanent beds on the
contour; 104 Site Repair — beds on the best ground, the tunnel, cellar,
and compost bays on the poorer; 147 Communal Eating — the garden serves
one table first; 170 Fruit Trees — where the toilet stream goes, beside
the beds and never in them. In tension with: 178 Compost — Alexander
joins toilets and kitchen waste into one fertiliser; here the two
streams are kept apart, and only the garden's own stream reaches the
beds. 172 Garden Growing Wild — permanent beds are formal ground; the
tension is held at the edges, and by keeping no earth bare. 4
Agricultural Valleys — the best ground here may be hayfield, and a bed
there is taken from winter fodder. 46 Market of Many Shops — the region
is reached through an association of growers and eaters, not a market.

The frost-free period is read from the literature and not from the

Fortier's garden, from which the bed system and several figures below
are taken, is in southern Quebec, hardiness zone 5, on gravelly loam
(pp. 45, 147). He sets the ideal site on a gentle, steady south-facing
slope under 5 percent, and never at the bottom of a slope or valley,
where frost comes earlier (pp. 22–24). In a mountain valley that points
the garden up the slope and away from the valley floor — toward the
tension between garden and hayfield named in Section 1.

## 3. Sizing method

v0.1 sized the garden from the kitchen's demand. This version sizes it
from the ground — about 3,000 m² — and then reads the result against the
demand. Both directions are kept: the ground sets what can be grown,
the demand sets what must be.

**What you must measure yourself before using this module:**

- **Your season** — the last spring and first autumn frost, from the
  nearest station for as many years as it holds, then from your own
  thermometer at bed height.
- **Your soil** — a laboratory analysis of pH, humus, and N, P, K, one
  sample for each kind of ground you mean to use. Fortier aims for pH
  6.5 and raises it with limestone in small doses, measured before each
  application (p. 58).
- **Your ground** — the area of deep, sunny ground that is not needed
  as hayfield, its slope and aspect, and the length of bed the contour
  allows.
- **Your water** — the flow of the stream that refills the pond, in its
  driest month; never the spring.
- **Your demand** — the kitchen's vegetables per person per day, and
  the days of demand in a year, residents included.

**The bed.** Width 0.75 m and paths 0.45 m, 1.2 m from centre to
centre: Fortier's 30-inch bed and 18-inch path, which he recommends to
anyone growing without a tractor because the tools for market gardening
are now made to that width (p. 34). Length 20 m, [set], as the contour
allows; Fortier's 100-foot beds suit his site and are, in his words,
not a standard — what matters is that every bed is the same length, so
that tarps, drip lines, and covers are cut once and fit every bed
(pp. 34–35). One bed is 15 m².

**The plot.** Sixteen beds side by side, with 1.8 m at each end for a
harvest cart, after Fortier's plots of sixteen beds with 6 feet at
either end (p. 35). One plot is 16 × 1.2 m by 20 + 2 × 1.8 m, 19.2 by
23.6 m, about 453 m².

**The rotation.** Six plots, [set], after Fortier's ten (pp. 66–69).
Each year three plots carry a heavy-feeding group and its compost, and
three carry greens and roots; the next year they change places. The
three groups, as in his rotation, are the Solanaceae; the Brassicaceae
with the Cucurbitaceae, which he combined in the same way; and the
alliums with garlic. Peas and beans go with greens and roots and take
no compost. Each group comes back to the same plot every sixth year.
The tunnel is outside the rotation.

**Our example:** garden_area, about 3,000 m², [set]; capacity, 34
people; use_days_per_year, 120; vegetables_per_person, 0.44 kg;
garden_share, 1.0, [set]; bed_yield, 2 kg per m². Values from the site
parameters file, held in the workshop.

**The output against the demand.** At the assumed yield, the 96 beds
give about 2,880 kg a season against a demand of about 1,795 kg: the
demand needs about 60 beds, and the other 36 carry the residents'
demand, the crops that do not reach the yield, and the years that go
badly. The difference is not a surplus. The association's second circle
opens only on a surplus measured through a full season, never on this
arithmetic.

**The compost.** Fortier's programme gives heavy feeders 5 wheelbarrows
of compost to a bed of 30 inches by 100 feet, each bed once every two
years; his wheelbarrows hold 5 cubic feet (p. 56). That is about 3.0 cm
of compost at each application. To build his beds he added about 7
wheelbarrows of a compost mix to each bed, about 4.3 cm (p. 45). Charles
Dowding's no-dig figures are higher: 5 cm to start, 3 cm a year after,
which would be about 72 m³ and 43 m³ here. Fortier advises beginning
growers to buy their compost rather than make it (p. 62). The garden's
own residues and the kitchen's scraps will not make 62 m³ in the first
year; the difference is bought in or built up over seasons, and the
compost module says how.

**The water.** Fortier gives most crops about 1¼ inches of water a
week, about 32 mm (p. 39), and advises a pond, lake, or river once more
than an acre is cultivated (p. 26). At 1,560 m² of bed and tunnel that
is about 50 m³ in a week without rain. The spring is sized for people —
2.55 m³ a day at capacity in the water module — and is never used for
the garden. The rainwater cistern of 4.2 m³ in the source working paper
was sized for a 50 m² nursery. Neither carries the garden.

**The pond.** Decided 2026-09-28: a small pond, always refilled by the
stream, carries the garden. 50 m³ a week is about 7 m³ a day, or about
5 L a minute running day and night; a stream that gives at least that
in its driest month refills a dry week's water as fast as the garden
uses it, and the pond only has to hold a day or two of watering and the
depth the pump's intake needs. Its volume is [set] from the stream's
measured flow. Fortier's advice for the pump: set it beside the water,
since pumps push well and pull badly; fit a sediment filter so the
emitters do not clog; and give the main line a relief valve (p. 39).
Because children are on the site, the pond is fenced with a gate and
shallow at its edges, as the sanitation working paper fences its own;
it is a reservoir, not a swimming pond.

The dolomite rate is 400 g per m², from a retailer's guide for loam at
pH 4.5 to 5.0; an agronomic table gives 4 to 5.5 t of lime a hectare,
as CaO, for loam at the same pH, once in six to eight years. The
analysis replaces both.

**Read the result against three others.** Fortier estimates 30 to 70
shares of a 20-week programme for each half acre of intensive garden
(p. 21); 3,000 m² is about three quarters of an acre, or about 44 to 104
shares. Alexander's Vegetable Garden gives one-tenth of an acre for a
family of four; 3,000 m² at that rate feeds about 30 people. Our example
gives about 1,440 m² of bed for 34 people's 120 days of demand, with a
margin. The comparisons say the size is not far out; they do not say it
is right.

**From beds to a crop plan.** Fortier plans the season from the demand
backwards: the number of times each single-harvest crop is wanted, the
units a week each multiple-harvest crop must give, and his table of
yield per 100-foot bed, which he says must be scaled to other bed
lengths (pp. 134–141). A 20 m bed is about two thirds of his. The crop
plan is the kitchen's menu turned into beds, plot by plot, within the
rotation; it is written each winter and is not part of this module.

**Why drip, not sprinklers.** Fortier waters open beds with
micro-sprinklers and keeps drip for the tunnels and plastic mulch,
because drip lines must be lifted before every hoeing; he also writes
that drip uses water far more efficiently (p. 37). Here water is the
scarce thing, so the design keeps drip on the beds and a short line of
mini-sprinklers for keeping direct-seeded beds moist until they
germinate, [set].

**Why the tunnel and the covers.** Fortier puts his whole spring
production under row cover of about 19 g per m², which raises soil
temperature by 2 to 3 degrees and protects against frost and wind
(pp. 120–121). A short mountain season asks the same. Caterpillar
tunnels — movable, of pipe, rope, and old film — are his cheaper
complement to a fixed tunnel (pp. 122–123).

**Why a 2 m fence.** Fortier fences against deer with metal fencing six
feet high, and calls electric fencing the cheaper option when starting
or renting (pp. 35–36).

**The layout, as designed.** Six plots on the contour, each of sixteen
beds; the tunnel, the store, and the compost bays together on the
poorer ground at one side, as near the plots as the ground allows —
Fortier puts the tool shed, washing, and cold room at the centre of
the gardens, so that no plot is far from them (p. 31); the wicket
toward the kitchen and the double gate toward the track for
deliveries. Drawn as relationships only; the walk sets the rest.

## 11. Adaptation notes

The design is a seed, not a copy: a different place grows a different
form from it.

- **A drier valley:** the water decides the garden's size before the
  ground does; size the source first, then the beds.
- **A colder valley:** fewer open beds and more under cover; the tunnel
  and caterpillar tunnels first, and crops chosen to the measured
  season.
- **A smaller group or less ground:** fewer plots of the same shape;
  keep sixteen beds to a plot and the rotation in pairs; the fence and
  tunnel do not shrink in proportion, so the cost per bed rises.
- **A larger group or more ground:** more plots; past about an acre,
  Fortier's advice is a second grower, not a machine (pp. 20–21).
- **Neutral soil:** leave out the lime; keep the analysis.

## 13. What to check

- **The stream and the pond** — the stream's flow in its driest month,
  by bucket and stopwatch as the water module measures the spring; at
  least about 5 L a minute carries the full garden; the pond's volume
  set from it; the water-use permit; the pump, its power, and its
  circuit with the energy module. Until the flow is measured, the
  garden is sized by its water, not by its ground.
- **Establishment compost** — a dated price for about 62 m³ of compost
  or rotted manure, or the garden's own nutrient stream designed to
  supply it over seasons.
- **Seed, hoops, and tarps** — priced from a crop plan and the bed
  length.
- **The season** — frost_free_days from the nearest station, then the
  site's own record.
- **Altitude and climate** ranges for Section 2.
- **The soil** — the analysis, replacing the assumed loam and pH and
  the dolomite rate.
- **The ground** — the walk: whether about 3,000 m² of ground under 5
  percent, facing south to south-east and out of the frost pocket,
  exists without taking the best hayfield; the contour length that
  sets the bed; replaces the 220 m of fence and the 120 m of main line.
- **vegetables_per_person** — the kitchen's own menu, replacing the
  rational norm.
- **bed_yield** — the garden's own record, bed by bed, from the first
  season; Fortier's yields per bed (p. 141) scaled to 20 m beds for
  comparison.
- **garden_share** — set by the association and the kitchen.
- **Resident days of demand** — once resident households are known.
- **The grower and seasonal help** — months, hours, and pay, set with
  the association.
- **Resolution No. 305** — the portion tables read on
  zakon.rada.gov.ua, confirming our reading of 0.40 to 0.46 kg a day.
- **The Ukrainian row** — every instrument read and signed; the written
  enquiry to the district food-safety service.
- **Quotations** for the lines in Section 6, dated, to replace the
  listings.
- **From L2, once built:** build times and out-of-order consequences in
  Section 5; the grower's daily tasks in Section 7; early signs and
  repair costs in Section 8; Section 9.
