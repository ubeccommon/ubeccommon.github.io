*Two streams, kept apart. The toilet stream — buckets, biochar, sealed

## 1. Purpose

Compost for the market garden's beds — about 62 m³ to build them and
about 22 m³ a year after — from the garden's own stream, and bought in
for the share the site cannot make. It says how much the site can make,
how much must be bought, for how long, and where the bays may stand.

**Five rules.** Two streams kept apart: nothing from the toilets, and no
cooked food, plate waste, or grease-trap solids, reaches the garden
pile — those go to the toilet stream's sealed vessels and to trees.
Charged char only: char enters the pile as it is built, 10 to 30
percent by volume, and never goes raw onto a bed. Hot is logged, not
assumed: a pile counts as hot only when its thermometer record meets
the sanitising steps in Section 7; every other pile is cold compost, for
edges, paths, and perennials. Distance before convenience: bays, piles,
and the manure heap keep the distances in Section 4, measured on the
ground, and the fallback is a longer carry, never a shorter distance.
Compost never leaves the hamlet: it is made and used here, and neither
sold nor given away. The work is the grower's, paid; toloka is not the
name for work the compost depends on.

**Connections.** Depends on: the market garden module for the need —
its bed area, its compost depths, and its rotation; the Phase 1 walk,
with the spring, the stream, the garden's pond, and every dwelling and
place where children are marked, and the distances in Section 4
measured with a tape; the biochar module for charged char, a second
exit from the same kiln; the kitchen and dining module for raw scraps;
a dated local quotation for rotted manure or compost; the household's
decision on animals. Feeds: the market garden module, with finished
compost to the beds and a price for the compost it buys. Hands to the
terra preta module: cooked food, plate waste, and grease-trap solids,
which that module sizes into its vessels.

**Patterns.** Grows from: not yet named. How the valley bedded its
animals and fed its gardens, where the manure heap stood relative to
the house, the spring, and the garden, and whether anything like a
compost heap was kept apart from it, are questions for the elders
before the first bay is built. Feeds: not yet named. Resonates with:
177 Vegetable Garden — the bays beside the plots they feed; 104 Site
Repair — bays, heap, and char store on the poorer ground; 25 Access
to Water — the protective strip keeps every pile out of the belt
along the stream and the pond; 64 Pools and Streams — covered bays
on a firm base keep leachate from running water; 169 Terraced Slope
— a bay built level on a terrace; 120 Paths and Goals — the bays on
the path the kitchen already walks. In tension with: 178 Compost —
Alexander joins toilets and kitchen waste into one heap; here the
toilet stream goes to trees and only the garden stream reaches the
beds, and cooked food goes with the toilet stream. 74 Animals —
animals among people, where the law keeps a manure heap 20 m from any
dwelling and from places where children are. 172 Garden Growing Wild
— cold compost feeds the edges and is kept off the seed beds.

## 3. Sizing method

The need is fixed by the beds. The supply is what the site's own
stream can make. The difference is bought. All three are read in
cubic metres of finished compost.

**What you must measure yourself before using this module:**

- **Your need** — the bed area and the compost depths you mean to use,
  from your own garden plan.
- **Your kitchen's raw scraps** — weighed for a full cooking week, raw
  plant material only, kept apart from cooked food and plate waste.
- **Your garden's residues** — what leaves the beds for the pile, not
  what stays under the tarp, weighed at each clearing, from the bed
  record.
- **Your manure** — the animals you keep or can take from, their
  stall days, and the bedding used.
- **Your distances** — spring, stream, pond, dwellings, kitchen, and
  places where children are, measured with a tape from the proposed
  bay, on the walk.
- **Your price** — a dated quotation for rotted manure or compost,
  delivered, from the nearest supplier.

**Our example:** capacity, 34 people; use_days_per_year, 120;
vegetables_per_person, 0.44 kg; garden_share, 1.0; prep_refuse_share,
7 to 31 percent; residue_to_pile, 0.2, [assumed]; compost_volume_ratio,
0.33 to 0.75; rotted_manure_density, 900 kg per m³. Values from the
site parameters file, held in the workshop. The bed area, 1,440 m², and
the compost depths are the market garden module's.

| Finished compost | Calculation | Our example |
|---|---|---|
| From the kitchen | 0.5–2.9 m³ × 0.33–0.75 | 0.2–2.2 m³ |
| From the garden | 1.9–11.5 m³ × 0.33–0.75 | 0.6–8.6 m³ |
| **Site-made, a year** | sum | **0.8–10.8 m³** |

**The kitchen.** The 1,795 kg is the vegetables the kitchen serves in
a year at capacity, net, from the market garden's demand line. What is
peeled and trimmed away before serving is added to it: prep_refuse_share
runs from 7 percent of the purchased weight for onion to 31 percent for
headed cabbage in FAO's tables (Chatfield, Food composition tables for
international use, 1953, Table 2), and from 12 to 30 percent in the
USDA's yields for school kitchens (Food Buying Guide, Section 2). Net
to gross: refuse = net × share ÷ (1 − share). Food waste is taken at
275 kg per m³ (US EPA, Volume-to-Weight Conversion Factors, 2016). The
resident households' scraps are not yet counted, [set].

**The garden.** The residue per m² runs from 1.0 kg for head lettuce to
5.5 kg for zucchini, fresh, in Lower Saxony's advisory values
(Landwirtschaftskammer Niedersachsen, 2007, Table 2). Most of it never
reaches the pile: the market garden cuts residues and covers them with
a black tarp on the bed, as Fortier does (Section 7 of that module),
and they feed the bed where they lie. residue_to_pile, 0.2, is the
share that leaves the bed — the bulky, the diseased, the tunnel's
haulm — and is [assumed] until the bed record gives it. Green garden
waste is taken at 138 to 148 kg per m³ loose (Minnesota PCA, 2021; US
EPA, 2016).

**The shrink.** compost_volume_ratio is the finished volume per raw
volume. The sources disagree: NC State gives a fall of 25 to 50 percent
(Composting on Organic Farms, 2006); the US EPA a pile that shrinks to
about one-third (Composting at Home); NDSU a fall of half to two-thirds
for manure (2011); Rynk a fall of one-quarter to more than one-half
(On-Farm Composting Handbook, 1992, p. 12). The range, 0.33 to 0.75, is
carried through; the site's own piles replace it.

**Char is not counted.** Charged char, 10 to 30 percent of the pile by
volume (Schmidt, Ithaka Institute), adds structure and holds
nutrients, and the finished pile is larger by that much. It is left
out of the supply above, because it feeds nothing on its own, and
because how much the kiln can give beyond the toilet stream's 1.5 m³ a
year is the biochar module's to say, [set].

### 3.3 What must be bought

| Year | Need | Site-made | Bought |
|---|---|---|---|
| Building the beds | 62 m³ | none finished yet | about 62 m³ |
| Each year after | 22 m³ | 0.8–10.8 m³ | about 11–21 m³ |

**In plain words.** The beds are built from bought compost or rotted
manure, all of it: nothing the site makes is finished before the first
beds are dug. After that, the site makes between a twenty-fifth and a
half of what the beds take, and the rest is bought — every year, for as
long as the garden runs at this size and the household keeps no
animals. It is a running cost, not a start-up cost. The gap closes only
by keeping animals, by taking a neighbour's manure, or by fewer beds.
Fortier advises beginning growers to buy their compost rather than make
it (p. 62), and buys his own from specialists (Kaminski, review in
Resilience, 2014).

### 3.4 If the household keeps animals

| Animal | Bedded manure, a year | Rotted, as m³ |
|---|---|---|
| One cow, 200 stall days | about 7 t | about 1.6–4.7 m³ |
| One sheep | about 0.5 t | about 0.1–0.3 m³ |

Bedded manure per head is from a Ukrainian agrochemistry textbook: about
7 t for a cow with 2 kg of straw a day over 200 stall days, about 0.5 t
for a sheep (Агрохімія, section on manure); a Vinnytsia agrarian
university guide gives cattle 4 to 13 t and sheep 0.4 to 1.2 t by the
length of the stall period. Rotting leaves 20 to 60 percent of the
mass (Rynk, 1992, p. 12); перегній weighs about 900 kg per m³
(Бондарчук, Lutsk NTU, practical class 2). A cow and a few sheep would
close a tenth to a half of the yearly gap; they would not close it.
The stall days in this valley are [set], and are for the household and
the elders [for the elders].

## 7. Operation and maintenance

Responsible: the grower, with a deputy — [from L2]. One named role,
paid; not a rota of residents.

- **Daily, in season:** raw kitchen scraps carried from the kitchen in
  lidded caddies and covered in the bay with dry material; cooked food,
  plate waste, and grease-trap solids never go to the bay — they go to
  the toilet stream's vessels.
- **As each pile is built:** greens and browns layered, about 30 parts
  carbon to 1 of nitrogen and about half water by weight, the ranges the
  University of California gives (Compost in a Hurry, 2001); charged
  char added through the pile; at least 0.9 m in each direction, the
  University of California's 36 inches, so that it can heat.
- **While a pile is hot:** the temperature read and written down each
  day at its centre. A pile counts as hot only when the record shows at
  least 3 days at 70 °C, 5 at 65 °C, 7 at 60 °C, or 14 at 55 °C — the
  sanitising steps of Regulation (EU) 2019/1009 — with turning to bring
  the outside in. Otherwise it is cold compost.
- **Seasonal:** hot piles built in the warm months; piles covered
  against rain and snow; the manure heap covered; bought manure stacked
  on arrival.
- **Annual:** the compost record — what went in, when, how hot, what
  came out — carried into the bed record and into prep_refuse_share,
  residue_to_pile, and compost_volume_ratio; the distances walked again.

Handling is by the grower and paid help. The toilet stream and its
open vessels belong to the terra preta module, whose rules hold for
them. If the programme works in the garden, that is described in the
programme modules, in standing format.

## 8. Failure modes

From the design, not from experience.

| Failure | Early sign | Repair and cost |
|---|---|---|
| Pile never heats | record under 55 °C | cold compost, edges only |
| Leachate toward water | [from L2] | cover, base, distance |
| Rats in the bays | [from L2] | mesh; no cooked food |
| Weed seed in bought manure | [from L2] | stack; a hot pile |
| Herbicide in bought manure | bean test fails | refuse the load |
| Raw manure on the beds | [from L2] | 120 days before harvest |
| Compost short of the beds | [from L2] | buy more, or fewer beds |
| Supplier too far to pay | [from L2] | a nearer source |

**Herbicide in bought manure.** Some grassland herbicides, aminopyralid
among them, pass through the animal and the heap and damage beans,
peas, potatoes, and tomatoes grown in the manure; the RHS advises a
simple test — beans sown in pots of the manure beside pots without —
before using manure of unknown origin (RHS, Weedkiller in manure). The
supplier is asked what the animals were fed.

**Raw manure.** No Ukrainian interval between raw manure and harvest
was found. The design takes 120 days before harvesting a crop that
touches the soil, and 90 for one that does not, from the US federal
rule on manure in crop production (7 CFR 205.203), [set], and keeps
raw manure off the beds altogether.

## 11. Adaptation notes

The design is a seed, not a copy: a different place grows a different
form from it.

- **A drier valley:** a pile dries before it heats; the covers matter
  more, and the pile is watered from the pond, never the spring.
- **A colder valley:** a shorter warm season means fewer hot piles and
  more cold compost; bought compost carries more of the beds.
- **A smaller group or less ground:** fewer beds, less need; the
  kitchen's share of the supply rises, and the bought share falls.
- **A larger group or more ground:** more beds, more need; the gap
  grows faster than the kitchen's scraps.
- **Animals kept:** manure becomes the largest stream; bedding and
  stall days decide it, and the heap keeps its distances.
- **Level ground far from water:** the siting table relaxes nothing,
  but is easy to meet; the bays stand where the path already goes.
