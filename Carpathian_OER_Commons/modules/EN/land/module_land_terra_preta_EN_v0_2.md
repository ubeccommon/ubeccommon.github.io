## 1. Purpose

The toilet stream made into soil for trees: everything that leaves the
bucket toilets, and the kitchen's cooked food, plate waste, and
grease-trap solids, sealed and fermented, worked by worms, and matured
a year, then used on trees, hedgerows, windbreaks, and nursery stock
only. Simple where it is handled, careful where it matters.

**Six rules.** One stream: nothing is separated; urine goes in with the
rest, and the kitchen's waste goes into the same vessels. Carbon holds
it: char and a carbon cover at every use, because char alone cannot
hold the urine. Ferment first, worms second, time last: sealed lactic
fermentation for at least six weeks, worm beds fed layered and bulked,
then a year in a closed bay. The clock counts from the last addition:
each bay is filled as a batch and closed, and its year runs from the
day it closes. Trees only: never on vegetable beds, never on anything
harvested raw within the season, and nothing in the month before a
fruit harvest. One responsible adult, paid: buckets, vessels, and worm
beds are handled by one named adult and a deputy, not a rota; the work
is a cost line, and toloka is not its name.

**Connections.** Depends on: the sanitation toilets row, which delivers
the full buckets and sets the char and cover at the seat; the biochar
module, for char; the compost module, which hands over cooked food,
plate waste, and grease-trap solids; the kitchen and dining module for
those volumes; the written answer of the district sanitary service;
the Phase 1 walk, with every distance in Section 4 measured with a
tape. Feeds: the trees, hedgerows, windbreaks, and the tree nursery
module's stock; the planting module. Rinse water from the buckets goes
to the septic tank, in the greywater module, never to the greywater
line.

**Patterns.** Grows from: not yet named. What this valley did with a
household's own waste, and whether anything of it was returned to the
land, are questions for the elders; the literature found nothing.
Feeds: not yet named. Resonates with: 178 Compost — toilets and
kitchen waste made into soil together, as Alexander asks, for the
trees; 170 Fruit Trees — trees, hedgerows, and nursery stock as the
ground the soil goes to; 104 Site Repair — store, beds, and bays on
the poorer ground; 25 Access to Water — the protective strip keeps
them out of the belt along the stream and the pond; 64 Pools and
Streams — a sealed base and covers keep a wet stream off running
water; 127 Intimacy Gradient — the store as the most withdrawn place
on the site. In tension with: 18 Network of Learning — the work is
shown only as far as the worm beds, and never the vessels; 120 Paths
and Goals — the daily route of the buckets is a working path, kept
short and apart from the day's movement.

## 3. Sizing method

**What you must measure yourself before using this module:**

- **Your bucket volume** — the full buckets of a session, measured by
  the litre as they are tipped, against the head count at capacity.
- **Your kitchen's waste** — cooked food, plate waste, and grease-trap
  solids, weighed for a full session.
- **Your cover** — how much cover a use takes to leave no smell, found
  in the first week, as Jenkins advises.
- **Your pH** — each vessel's pH at sealing and at opening, to set the
  starter and the carbohydrate.
- **Your receiving ground** — the trees, hedgerows, windbreaks, and
  nursery beds that can take a year's soil, counted on the walk.
- **Your distances** — as in Section 4, measured with a tape.

**Our example:** capacity, 34 people; bucket_share, two-thirds;
urine_per_person, 1.4 L a day; faeces_per_person, 0.128 kg a day;
char_at_seat, 0.5 L a day; cover_at_seat, 0.8 L a day, [assumed];
meals_per_day, 3, [assumed]; food_waste_per_portion, 58 to 129.5 g;
use_days_per_year, 120; compost_volume_ratio, 0.33 to 0.75. Values from
the site parameters file, held in the workshop.

### 3.1 What goes into the vessels, a day at capacity

| Stream | Calculation | Our example |
|---|---|---|
| People at the buckets | 34 × two-thirds | about 22.7 |
| Urine | 22.7 × 1.4 L | about 31.7 L |
| Faeces | 22.7 × 0.128 kg | about 2.9 L |
| Char at the seat | 22.7 × 0.5 L | about 11.3 L |
| Cover at the seat | 22.7 × 0.8 L | about 18.1 L |
| **Buckets, a day** | sum | **about 64 L** |
| Cooked food and plates | 102 × 58–129.5 g | 5.9–13.2 kg |
| Same, by volume | ÷ 737 kg per m³ | about 8–18 L |
| **Into the vessels, a day** | sum | **about 72–85 L** |

The upper figure takes the paper's faeces figure, 0.25 L a person,
instead of the median; the difference is under 3 L a day.

**Where the paper's figures change.** The paper counted faeces and char
only, about 19 L a day at 25 people. It left out the urine, which it
keeps in the stream (Section 3 of the paper) and which is the largest
part of it; it did not apply its own share of two-thirds of uses to the
buckets; and its faeces figure, 0.25 L a person a day, is about twice
the median of 128 g wet a person a day, at 75 percent water, in Rose
and colleagues' review (2015), which also gives 1.4 L of urine a person
a day as the median. Faeces are taken here at about 1 kg a litre,
[assumed].

**The cover.** Char holds about 1 to 4 mg of nitrogen a gram (Bischak
and colleagues, 2024) or less (Fidel and colleagues, 2018), and urine
carries a median 11 g of nitrogen a person a day (Rose and colleagues,
2015): no scoop of char can hold it. Joseph Jenkins's humanure practice
takes urine and faeces together under a carbon cover, and finds that
one 20 L receptacle serves one adult for a week with the right cover
(Humanure Sanitation, 2009) — about 2.9 L a person a day in all.
cover_at_seat, 0.8 L, is what is left of that after urine, faeces, and
char, and is [assumed] until the first week sets it. Sawdust, dry
leaves from the yard, or shredded straw.

**The kitchen.** Food service in Finland wasted 58 to 129.5 g a portion
across sectors, kitchen, serving, and plate together (Silvennoinen and
colleagues, Waste Management, 2019). Portions are capacity ×
meals_per_day. Kitchen waste is taken at 737 kg per m³ (Cornell Waste
Management Institute, cafeteria waste). Grease-trap solids are [set],
from the weekly cleaning, and kept a small share: worms are harmed
above about 5 percent fat (Wu and colleagues, 2019).

### 3.2 Vessels, worm beds, and bays

| Quantity | Calculation | Our example |
|---|---|---|
| A session, 20 days | 72–85 L × 20 | about 1.4–1.7 m³ |
| A year, 120 days | 72–85 L × 120 | about 8.7–10.2 m³ |
| Sealed, six weeks at peak | 85 L × 42 days | about 3.6 m³ |
| Vessels | 3.6 m³ ÷ 100 L | 36 vessels of 100 L |
| Each worm bed, 4 months | a third of a year | about 2.9–3.4 m³ |
| Soil out, a year | 8.7–10.2 m³ × 0.33–0.75 | about 2.9–7.6 m³ |
| Each bay, 6 months' soil | half a year out | about 1.4–3.8 m³ |

**The vessels.** A vessel is opened no earlier than six weeks after it
is sealed (paper, Section 10). The peak is sessions back to back: six
weeks of the upper figure is about 3.6 m³, which is 36 vessels of 100 L.
The paper's rule is to buy twice as many as the arithmetic suggests, so
that nothing is opened early; 36 meets it when sessions do not run back
to back, and is the minimum when they do. Fermented material keeps its
volume (paper, Section 4).

**The worm beds.** Three, in rotation, as the paper has them; each
takes about four months of the vessels' output, 2.9 to 3.4 m³ before
the bulking and the finished vermicompost it is layered with. Eisenia
fetida did not survive fresh faeces until they were layered with
vermicompost (Yadav and colleagues, 2010), and fermented faeces without
bulking were not accepted by worms (Factura and colleagues, 2010); the
bulking share is [set] from the first batches, and sets each bed's
size. Worms work best at 65 to 80 percent moisture (Lalander and
colleagues, 2013).

**The bays.** Each bay takes six months of what the worm beds release
and is then closed for a year: one filling, two maturing, three in
all, as the paper has them. What leaves the worm beds is taken at
compost_volume_ratio, 0.33 to 0.75, of what went in, until measured.

**How long.** Six weeks sealed, three to six months in the worms, up
to six months filling a bay, and a full year closed: a year from the
last addition, and up to about two years from the first. WHO gives 1.5
to 2 years at 2 to 20 °C for dry excreta with no new material added
(Guidelines for the Safe Use of Wastewater, Excreta and Greywater, Vol.
4, 2006); the fermentation and the worms reduce bacteria but are not a
barrier against Ascaris eggs (Harroff and colleagues, 2019; Andreev and
colleagues, 2017), so the time and the trees carry the safety.

### 3.3 What the stream asks of the site, a year

| Quantity | Calculation | Our example |
|---|---|---|
| Char at the seat | 11.3 L × 120 | about 1.4 m³ |
| Cover at the seat | 18.1 L × 120 | about 2.2 m³ |
| Carbohydrate, [assumed] | 10 percent of the mass | about 870–1,020 kg |
| Soil to the trees | Section 3.2 | about 2.9–7.6 m³ |

**The carbohydrate.** With urine in the stream, its buffering holds
the pH up. A laboratory study reached pH 4.1 to 5.2 only with at least
10 percent carbohydrate as molasses equivalent, and found char made
little difference (Hübner and Moeller, Fermentation, 2026); a field
trial on pit sludge used 10 percent molasses with 10 percent milk
culture (Anderson and colleagues, 2015). The 10 percent of the mass
sealed is [assumed], vessel contents taken at 1 kg a litre, and the
kitchen's own starch counts toward it; the pH record replaces it.

## 7. Operation and maintenance

Responsible: one named adult, paid, with a deputy — [from L2]. Not a
rota.

- **Daily, in session:** buckets exchanged by the responsible adult and
  taken to the store on the cart; tipped into the open vessel; the
  vessel topped with char and cover and closed; buckets rinsed at the
  dedicated point, the rinse to the septic tank; the kitchen's cooked
  food and plate waste into the same vessel, covered.
- **As each vessel fills:** starter and carbohydrate added, pH read,
  the vessel sealed and labelled with the date; not opened for six
  weeks.
- **Weekly:** the store checked; the grease-trap solids added in small
  measure; the char and cover stocks checked.
- **Monthly:** worm beds fed in thin layers of fermented material with
  bulking and finished vermicompost; moisture felt and kept between 65
  and 80 percent; beds kept in strict rotation.
- **Seasonal:** worm beds covered and banked before frost; a bay closed
  and labelled with its closing date when it has taken six months.
- **Annual:** a bay opened only a full year after its closing date;
  the soil taken to trees, hedgerows, windbreaks, and nursery stock,
  never to a vegetable bed, and not in the month before a fruit harvest;
  the batch record kept — sealed, opened, bedded, closed, applied.

The making of the soil is visible and explicable; the handling stays
with the responsible adult. The worm beds may be shown; open vessels
are not, and the store stays locked. How the programme relates to this
work is described in the programme modules, in standing format.

## 11. Adaptation notes

The design is a seed, not a copy: a different place grows a different
form from it.

- **A drier valley:** less cover is needed to hold the stream; the worm
  beds need watering, from rainwater, never the spring.
- **A colder valley:** the store and the worm beds are the weak points;
  more lining, beds against a heated wall, and a longer year.
- **A smaller group:** fewer vessels, the same three beds and bays,
  smaller; the store shrinks with the vessels.
- **A larger group:** vessels, beds, and bays scale with Section 3; the
  receiving ground must grow with them.
- **Plumbed units required:** the bucket figures fall with
  bucket_share; the kitchen's share stays.
- **Urine diverted, where a site chooses it:** the vessels take a third
  of the volume, and the cover and the carbohydrate fall with it.
