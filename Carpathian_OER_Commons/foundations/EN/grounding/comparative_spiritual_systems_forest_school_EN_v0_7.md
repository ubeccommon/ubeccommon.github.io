*Companion document to "The Eco-Spiritual Philosophy of the Living
Forest." It surveys spiritual systems and worldviews that the Forest
School learns from. The rule of the survey is stated at its end and
governs throughout: the school honours these traditions by parallel, never
by imitation; the local tradition leads; nothing is transplanted, and
every source is named.*

## I. The Taproot — The Hutsul and Ukrainian Folk Worldview

The philosophy of the Living Forest grows from this ground. The Hutsul
highlands treat the land as something to live with and respect: mountains,
springs, and trees as presences, the high pasture a lived-with world,
never a wilderness apart. Hutsul night vigils once held games, jokes, and
songs beside the dead, so that no one kept the night alone. Around it lies
the Ukrainian folk year, kept for centuries within the life of the Church:
the Green Feasts, when homes and churches fill with branches, the Kupala
fires and water customs, songs that bless the field and the year. What the
school draws, and how, is for the elders of the valley to say. [for the
elders]

## II. The Churches of This Valley

The Orthodox and Greek-Catholic churches that hold the Carpathians carry a
deep theology of creation: the world as icon of its maker, blessed with
water and branch through the year's feasts; the hesychast practice of
stillness and watchfulness of heart; in the Orthodox world, the Ecumenical
Patriarch Bartholomew's long teaching on care for creation; and in the
wider church, from Francis of Assisi, who called the creatures brother and
sister, to the encyclical Laudato Si' on care for our common home, a green
thread running through the church's history. What the school draws: the
families' own feasts — the Theophany water-blessing, the Green Feasts, the
harvest — keep their place in the school's year; children keep their
Sundays with their families; the parish priest is welcome at any time. The
school stands beside the faith of the families it serves, never against it
and never in its place.

## III. The Old Ways of Northern and Eastern Europe

Before the survey crosses the oceans, it crosses the Baltic, where the old
European bond to place lies close to the surface and survived long in the
open.

**The Baltic peoples — Lithuania, Latvia, and the old Prussian coast.**
Lithuania was the last state in medieval Europe to accept Christianity: it
was baptised in 1387, Samogitia only in 1413, and the old ways never fully
left. Sacred groves and holy oaks stood at the heart of worship — the oak
as the thunder-god's tree, felled only at peril — and the world was sung:
the dainas, the vast body of Latvian folk quatrains, over two hundred
thousand collected, with the Lithuanian dainos their close kin, hold the
sun, the seasons, the field-work, and the soul's passages, a whole
cosmology kept alive in song. In living memory the singing held: the
Singing Revolution of 1987 to 1991 met an empire with massed song and,
largely without violence, won back independence. What the school draws:
company for its own practice — that a people's bond to land can be *kept
in song* across centuries of pressure, and that singing together can be
survival and resistance. The valley's own singing and the dainas sound, to
this school, like kin.

**The Finnic north — Finland, Karelia, Estonia.** In the Kalevala songs,
the sage does not fight the world into shape; he *sings* it, and power
over a thing comes from knowing and singing its origin. The Estonian hiis,
sacred groves, are tended to this day, and the old reverence for the bear
honoured even its skull, set in a tree. The bond lives on as everyman's
right — the freedom of every person to walk, gather, and be at home in the
land, paired with the duty to leave it unharmed — written into Estonian
and Swedish law, and in Finland a custom the law upholds. What the school
draws: that to know a being's origin is to be in relationship with it —
every pattern card asking "what is this, and where does it come from"
walks the singer's path — and that belonging to land can be both right and
care.

**The Norse world.** The cosmos was a tree: Yggdrasil, its roots reaching
to the wells of fate and wisdom and down into the cold deep, its branches
spread over heaven and earth. The land was alive with landvættir, the land
spirits: ships approaching home shores took down their dragon-head prows
so as not to frighten them — arrival made gentle before the spirits of
place. Law was spoken under the open sky, the assembly gathered outdoors
on the land it governed. And wisdom, in the deepest Norse story, was won
on the tree. What the school draws: the tree as a readable cosmos, which
the school's children learn to read in their own way, as roots, trunk, and
canopy; the practice of arriving gently on any land; and the circle under
open sky as an old form of assembly. One Norse path the school honours and
does not walk: the solitary vigil and the ordeal on the tree belong to
that tradition's adults and stay there.

**The Celts.** The grove — nemeton — was a sanctuary; wells and springs
were doorways of the holy; the year turned on threshold festivals, Samhain
and Bealtaine. Roman writers say the learned orders trained for as much as
twenty years, carrying wisdom wholly in memory and verse, and one places
their teaching in caves and remote groves. In ogham, several letters bear
trees' names, and medieval Irish scholars read the whole alphabet as
trees, so that to learn one's letters was to learn the forest. Early Irish
law ranked the trees in classes and fined their wounding — nobles of the
wood, protected by statute. What the school draws: an old European
precedent for teaching in and through the forest — a curriculum read in
trees, thresholds kept with ceremony, and the law standing guard over the
wood.

## IV. The Peoples of the Urals, Taiga, Tundra, and Steppe

East of the old European ways, across the Urals and into the taiga and
tundra, live peoples in their own lands: Mari El on the middle Volga;
Udmurtia and Komi west of the Urals; Bashkortostan in their south; the
Khanty-Mansi lands on the Ob; the Nenets tundra of Yamal; the Evenki
taiga; Sakha (Yakutia); Altai, Tuva, and Buryatia. These are the
traditions of peoples, never of an empire. Their languages and religions
were suppressed under the empire and in the Soviet decades. The school
speaks of their traditions only as they have been shared in the open, and
does not speak for them or for their politics.

**The Mari.** In Mari El, Mari communities gather in several hundred
küsoto, sacred groves, for prayers led by priests called karts — a faith
suppressed in Soviet times and practised openly today, one of Europe's
last grove-faiths kept in the open. What the school draws: the knowledge
that a grove-faith is still practised in the open in this decade — the
hiis and the nemeton not only as memory but as neighbour.

**The Udmurt.** In Udmurtia, families kept kuala, household shrines, and
villages kept lud, sacred groves; the village religion is still kept. What
the school draws: the sacred at household scale — reverence a homestead
can live.

**The Komi.** In Komi, where one of Europe's largest remaining virgin
forests stands, the forest had its own spirit, and the Perm animal-style
bronzes of the Komi and Udmurt ancestors show human and animal as one
figure. What the school draws: what reverence can keep.

**The Bashkirs.** In Bashkortostan, the epic of Ural-batyr tells of a hero
who gives the water of life to the land instead of drinking it; the stones
his people heaped on his grave became the Ural Mountains. Beekeeping in
the hollows of wild forest trees is still kept there. What the school
draws: a life given so that the land lives, and a covenant of people,
tree, and bee.

**The Khanty and Mansi.** The Ob-Ugrian peoples of the Khanty-Mansi lands
— the closest linguistic kin of the Hungarians, and more distant kin of
the Finns and Estonians — hold the bear in deepest reverence. Their bear
festival, held when a bear has been killed, runs through days and nights
of sacred songs, stories, and performances; it was banned in Soviet times.
What the school draws: a resemblance, not a shared origin — another
community answering awe and death with song, story, and play, as the
valley's vigils once did.

**The Nenets.** On the Yamal tundra, Nenets herding families move with the
reindeer: land as partner, the herd as calendar, belonging carried in
movement rather than walls. What the school draws: a teaching it learns
from — that belonging can be carried in movement as well as held in walls.

**The Evenki.** The Evenki of the central Siberian taiga gave the word
"shaman" to European languages, through Russian; outsiders then carried it
to many peoples whose practices it does not fit. What the school draws:
nothing it practises. The school does not use the word for its own work.

**The Sakha, and the peoples of Altai, Tuva, and Buryatia.** In Sakha
(Yakutia), the epic Olonkho — inscribed by UNESCO among the world's
intangible heritage — sets the cosmos around Aal Luuk Mas, the great world
tree, and the ysyakh festival at midsummer greets the sun at its height
and the year's renewal. In Altai, Tuva, and Buryatia, the eternal sky is
revered above sacred peaks, travellers add stones to the oboo cairns at
passes, and in khoomei throat-singing a single voice sounds the river, the
wind, and the mountain at once. What the school draws: another reading of
the cosmos as a tree; small acts of reverence at the thresholds of travel;
and the company of peoples whose land is held in song.

## V. North America — Named Nations

No single worldview covers the hundreds of distinct nations of North
America. Turtle Island is the name many nations of the northeast give the
land; it is not every nation's name. The school listens to teachings those
nations have chosen to share publicly, and names them by nation.

**The Thanksgiving Address (Haudenosaunee).** The "words before all else",
Ganonhanyonh: at the opening and closing of gatherings, greetings and
thanks are spoken to the people, the waters, the plants, the animals, the
winds, the sun, moon, and stars — until the minds of those gathered are
one. It is a living practice, spoken anew by each speaker. What the school
draws: its Morning Circle is its own, and it knows the Address came first;
the school does not recite it.

**The generations to come (Haudenosaunee).** Decisions weighed for the
generations yet unborn — in the Great Law's words, "those whose faces are
yet beneath the surface of the ground"; Haudenosaunee teachers today speak
of looking seven generations ahead. What the school draws: planting trees
for children not yet born, and handing a record forward, stand beside this
principle, credited to the Haudenosaunee.

**All My Relations (Lakota: mitákuye oyás'iŋ).** The prayer-word that
names the whole world as family: every being related, the human one
relative among many. What the school draws: its refusal of "wilderness",
and a circle of care in which forest, child, elder, and community belong
together.

**The Honourable Harvest (as taught by Robin Wall Kimmerer, Potawatomi).**
Kimmerer calls it nearly universal among peoples who live close to the
land. Never take the first; never take more than half; ask permission; use
everything taken; give a gift in return for what is given. What the school
draws: its ethic for every act of gathering — seed, branch, water, even
knowledge from an elder — taking with asking, using with respect,
returning more than was taken.

## VI. Teachings from Other Lands

**Aboriginal nations of Australia — Country and songlines.** Country is
kin: a living relative to be cared for, not a resource. Knowledge of land
is held in songlines — routes walked and sung, where the song carries the
map and the law. Songlines belong to their custodians, and much of that
knowledge is restricted. What the school draws: respect, not method. The
pattern language is the school's own and claims no kinship with them.

**Aotearoa — the Māori way.** Whakapapa binds people and the natural world
into one genealogy — the forest is ancestor, not property; kaitiakitanga
names the duty of guardianship that flows from that kinship. In
settlements negotiated by Tūhoe and by Whanganui iwi, New Zealand law
recognises Te Urewera and the Whanganui River as legal persons. What the
school draws: guardianship as inherited duty, named in the school's own
words.

**The Andes — Pachamama and ayni.** The living earth-mother receives and
gives; ayni, sacred reciprocity, orders human life — help given must
circulate, gifts return as gifts. Sumak kawsay, the good life, means
living well *with*, never at the expense of. What the school draws: ayni
stands beside the toloka as kin, and places the school's economy of care
in a wider family of reciprocal economies.

**Sápmi — the Sámi way.** Some Sámi families herd reindeer; most Sámi live
by other work, and fishing and hunting came long before herding became a
whole way of life. The joik does not sing *about* a person, animal, or
place — it sings them present. It was long condemned by missionaries, and
was banned in schools in the Sámi area as late as the 1950s. What the
school draws: song as a way of holding beings and places in presence —
company for the valley's own singing.

**Ubuntu, and the sacred groves of West Africa.** Ubuntu, from the Nguni
proverb umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu: a person is a person through other
persons — being itself is relational. The Yoruba sacred grove of
Osun-Osogbo has been kept for centuries and, when that care failed in the
1950s, restored; it is probably the last of many that once stood outside
Yoruba settlements. What the school draws: its deepest sentence — care
circulating until no one is only giver or receiver — is ubuntu extended to
the land, an extension that is ours, not the proverb's; and the grove
shows that reverence can keep a forest for centuries, and that it has not
always been enough.

## VII. The Asian Ways

**Shinto (Japan), and shinrin-yoku.** Nature as sacred space alive with
kami. Shinrin-yoku, "forest bathing", is a name given in 1982 by Japan's
forestry agency to quiet time among trees; research on its effects is
young. What the school draws: sensory immersion as practice — silent walks
in which the child's attention rests on the trees around them — the tenet
of Bathing in Life.

**Daoism (China).** The living flow of the world; naturalness; wu-wei,
action through non-striving; water and softness as true strength. What the
school draws: its manner — patient observation, adaptation to rhythm, the
forest and the child each allowed to work at their own pace; the
gardener's restraint as a discipline.

**Buddhism.** Mindfulness as bare attention; interbeing — Thich Nhat
Hanh's word for the older teaching of dependent origination: nothing
exists separately, the tree in the paper, the cloud in the tree;
impermanence held without despair. What the school draws: the quality of
attention in its observation practices, and the composting movement's
deepest logic — that what passes feeds what comes, and letting go is a
form of care.

**The Vedic and village traditions of India.** Sacred groves (devrai and
their many regional names) protected as the gods' own; ahimsa, non-harm,
as the first ethic; and in living memory the Chipko movement, in which
villagers — at Reni in 1974, the village women led by Gaura Devi —
embraced trees with their bodies to stop the axes. What the school draws:
the knowledge that people who defend a forest stand in a long line of
ordinary people whose care became protection.

## VIII. Judaism and Islam

**Judaism.** Bal tashchit — the command not to destroy wantonly, rooted in
the Torah's protection of fruit trees even in war (Deuteronomy 20:19–20);
shmita, the sabbath of the land, a rest every seventh year; Tu BiShvat,
the new year of the trees, kept with planting since the 1890s. What the
school draws: rhythm as sacred law — even the land must rest — and one of
the oldest written protections of trees in war, set down some two and a
half thousand years before this one.

**Islam.** The human as khalifa — trustee, not owner, of the earth; mizan,
the balance set in creation that must not be transgressed; the teaching,
in the hadith, that one who plants a tree from which any creature eats has
given charity. What the school draws: stewardship as trusteeship — the
land held in care for its true owner and for those to come — and the
planting of a tree as an act of generosity that outlives the planter.

## IX. The Modern Convergence

**Biophilia and eco-spirituality.** The hypothesis, named by E. O. Wilson
in 1984, that humans carry an innate need for connection with living
systems; and the growing practice of spirituality expressed through care
rather than dogma. What the school draws: its plainest statement — hands
in the soil — speakable in the language of research as well as of
reverence.

**Deep ecology.** The philosophical position that the living world has
worth in itself, not only as human resource, and that the self, fully
understood, extends into the web of relations that sustain it. What the
school draws: a bridge language for partners who arrive through philosophy
rather than faith.

**The rights of nature.** From Ecuador's constitution of 2008 to the
settlements in Aotearoa, some courts and legislatures now treat the living
world as a subject, not only an object. What the school draws: company.
That the law of some states now says this is not evidence that the
school's practice works.

## X. Shapes That Recur

Read together, the traditions do not blur into sameness. Read by this
school, for its own purposes, some shapes recur, and the school finds
company in them:

- **Kinship, not wilderness.** Many of these worldviews refuse the cut
  between human and nature: relations, ancestors, Country, kami, creation
  — never "environment."
- **Reciprocity as law.** Ayni, toloka, the Honourable Harvest, ubuntu,
  the trust of the khalifa: taking obliges giving; care must circulate.
- **Gratitude as protocol.** Not a mood but a practice with a form — words
  before all else, grace, blessing, the morning circle.
- **Land as teacher.** Country, pattern languages, the ogham letters (a
  few of them named for trees), the tree read as roots, trunk, and canopy:
  the ground itself as curriculum.
- **Song and ceremony where words fail.** Grief, threshold, and joy
  carried in the body — vigil, joik, lament, dance — in many of the
  traditions above.
- **Long time.** The generations yet unborn, shmita cycles, groves kept
  for centuries: traditions that think in tree-time.
- **The accompanied journey.** Into the forest and back, with an adult
  alongside; the child comes home with something to share. An old shape of
  learning, found in many places.

That some such shapes recur from the Carpathians to the Baltic and across
the Urals, from North America to Aotearoa, says something about people. It
is not evidence for the school's philosophy, which the school holds as
ground. It is good company.

## XII. The Ethic of Borrowing

One rule governs the school's relationship to every tradition in this
survey, and it is absolute:

The school honours by parallel, never by imitation. It does not perform
the ceremonies of other peoples, does not adopt their sacred forms, and
does not blend their practices into an unrooted mixture. It practises its
*own* place's forms — Hutsul and Ukrainian, under the elders' authority —
and recognises, with gratitude and by name, the traditions of the world in
which it sees something of its own practice. Where a teaching from
elsewhere informs the school's understanding, it is credited to its
people; where a practice belongs to a people, it stays with them. The
survey above is a family album, not a catalogue of parts. The school's
answer to the world's wisdom is not to borrow its songs but to sing, more
faithfully, its own.

## Source check and stress tests

Done 2026-09-27, by readers who had not seen the text being written.

**Source check.** Eighty-three factual claims tested against published
sources: fifty-two confirmed, six wrong, twenty overstated, five
unverified. The wrong ones: the Hutsuls placed in the Finno-Ugric world;
the Khanty and Mansi as the closest kin of Finnish and Estonian; the law
on fruit trees dated three thousand years back; every ogham letter a tree;
the Sámi as a reindeer people; the Honourable Harvest as a teaching of one
nation. All are corrected above, with the overstated and unverified
claims.

**A reader from the named peoples.** Found peoples used as proof of the
school's worth; a Māori role-name and a kinship with songlines taken; the
Evenki crossing half-practised; the Sámi reduced to herders; Turtle Island
used for every nation. Found also that since November 2024, when Russia's
Supreme Court listed 172 Indigenous organisations as terrorist, calling
these peoples Ukraine's kin can be used against them. All corrected.

**A parent, a priest, and an official in wartime Ukraine.** Found no plain
statement that nothing here is done with children; animism set as the
ground with the Church as a later layer; the valley's churches placed
seventh; the peoples of the Urals and Siberia read politically. Corrected:
the note for families; the churches placed after the taproot; the Hutsul
section rewritten for the elders; the section on the Urals and Siberia
kept, by Michel Garand's decision, with each people named by its own land
and spoken of only as it has shared its traditions.

**An evidence reviewer.** Found conviction presented as evidence
throughout, against this survey's own opening note and the foundations
paper, and the move that treats many traditions as one deep core,
criticised by Steven Katz ("Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism", 1978).
Corrected: the traditions are held as company, not evidence.
