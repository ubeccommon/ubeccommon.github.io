## I. The Child at the Centre

Everything in this philosophy is measured first by one thing: the
transformation within the child. The forest is regenerated, trees are
planted, springs are cleared — and all of it stands as the tangible,
physical reflection of an inner healing, never as an abstract goal beside
it. The natural world acts as co-therapist and mirror: what the child
tends outwardly is what mends inwardly.

Five movements of the child's recovery order the work:

- **Safety first.** Acute anxiety lowered; basic trust rebuilt; a child's
  inner sense of agency, groundedness, and personal boundary restored.
  Nothing else begins until the ground of safety holds.
- **The senses reopened.** War numbs. Recovery begins as sensory
  reawakening — the texture of soil, pine scent on the wind, stillness
  that no longer threatens, joy relearned through the body before it is
  spoken.
- **Purpose and identity redefined.** The child's self-perception moves
  from "victim of war" to creator, defender, and steward of life on their
  native land. This is not asked of the child; it grows from what the
  child does and sees themselves doing.
- **Faith in light and goodness restored.** Inner resilience grows through
  belonging to something larger than oneself — living, enduring, and held
  sacred by the community around the child.
- **A flourishing forest as living proof.** Ecological regeneration is the
  derived outcome and the visible testimony: the forest thrives, and its
  thriving tells the child a truth no words could carry — *you have the
  power to nurture, to heal, and to give life back to the world.*

The school's part in this is the gardener's part. The programme heals no
one and teaches no one in the old sense; it prepares ground, keeps rhythm,
and accompanies. The healing is done by the forest, the circle, and the
child, at the child's own pace.

## II. Roots in the Wisdom of the World

The philosophy is grounded in deep human wisdom and experience, drawn
together in a contemporary, non-dogmatic synthesis — and ordered so that
the local tradition leads and the world's traditions stand beside it as
kin.

**The Hutsul worldview** is the taproot. The highlands hold a deep animism
— mountains, waters, and trees as living presences — and a tradition that
meets death itself with community, games, laughter, and song: the night
vigils in which grief is confronted together until the fear of death is
defeated. From this the school draws its boldest medicine: grief
transformed through archaic games, polyphonic singing, ritual dance, and
the celebration of the eternal cycle of life. [for the elders]

**The ways of the Indigenous nations of North America** — many nations,
each distinct, whose publicly shared teachings the school receives with
gratitude and by name. From the Haudenosaunee: the Thanksgiving Address,
words of gratitude spoken before all else until the minds of the gathered
are one, and decisions weighed for the generations yet unborn. From the
Lakota: *mitákuye oyás'iŋ* — all my relations — the whole living world
named as family. From the Honourable Harvest, as taught by the Potawatomi
botanist Robin Wall Kimmerer: never take the first, never take more than
half, ask permission, use everything taken, give a gift in return. What
the school draws: its morning circle of gratitude, its planting for
children who will never meet the planters, its refusal of the word
"wilderness," and its ethic for every act of taking stand beside these
ways, credited to them — a debt the school names openly and with respect.
The companion survey, *The Forest in the World's Wisdom*, holds the fuller
account.

**The old ways of Europe's north and east, and of the peoples of the Urals
and Siberia,** stand beside the valley as company. The Baltic peoples kept
a whole cosmology alive in song — the dainas — and in living memory sang
their way back to independence; the Mari of Mari El still keep their
sacred groves in the open; the Khanty and Mansi of the Ob answer the
killing of a bear with days and nights of sacred song and story, a
resemblance to the valley's own vigils; and in the Bashkir epic of
Bashkortostan the hero gives the water of life to the land itself, and the
stones heaped on his grave became the Ural Mountains. These are named as
peoples, in their own lands, never as an empire, and the companion survey
holds their fuller account.

**The Japanese forest way** — nature as sacred space, alive with kami,
and, since 1982, shinrin-yoku, forest bathing, quiet time among trees —
gives the school its practice of sensory immersion: silent walks, the
trees met as living beings.

**The Daoist way** — the living flow of things, naturalness, action
through non-striving — gives the school its manner: learning through
patient observation, adapting to life's rhythms, softness cultivated as
true inner strength. The forest is allowed to work at its own pace, and so
is the child.

**Biophilia and eco-spirituality** — the innate human need for connection
with living systems, spirituality expressed through care rather than dogma
— give the school its plainest creed: a practical faith rooted in action,
hands in the soil, direct responsibility for a piece of the homeland.

Beneath them all runs the oldest human pattern: across cultures and ages,
people crossed out of the settled world into the forest — accompanied by a
guide, an elder, a healer — and returned changed, carrying something the
community needed. The school keeps that shape and holds it gently:
accompanied crossing, threshold, return — without ordeal, and with every
child's own faith walking beside them unasked. And one rule governs every
kinship named here, as it governs the companion survey in full: the school
honours by parallel, never by imitation — it sings its own place's songs,
and greets the world's by name.

## III. The Tenets of the Living Forest

### Tenet 1 — The Forest as a Living Temple, Without Walls or Dogmas

The true temple is not built of stone; it lives. The forest accepts every
child unconditionally — without judgement, without asking about their
past, their trauma, or their beliefs. It offers sanctuary, silence, and
safety for renewal of body and spirit. And because the temple has no
walls, it has no doctrine: the school's convictions are lived in the open
where every family can see and choose them, and no belief is ever pressed
on a child. The temple is entered by putting hands in the soil.

### Tenet 2 — Carpathian Shinrin-Yoku: Bathing in Life

Reconnection begins in the five senses — and the child has far more than
five. War numbs the senses in layers, and the forest reopens them in
layers.

First, the senses of the body's own life: touch against rough bark;
balance regained on uneven mountain ground; the feel of one's own movement
in climbing, carrying, digging; and the quiet inner sense of wellbeing —
the felt knowledge *I am alive, and in this moment I am well* — which
terror silences first and the forest restores first.

Then, the senses that meet the world: pine breathed on the wind; spring
water tasted; the warmth of sun and of fire; light watched moving through
the canopy; mountain wind and birdsong heard in a silence that no longer
threatens.

And at last, the senses that only meet another person: hearing a voice,
understanding the thought that lives behind the words, perceiving the
living presence of another I. These are the senses war wounds through
sirens and betrayal — and the circle, the song, and the elders are how
they reopen.

In the school's learning architecture this whole sensorium is the ground
floor of everything: the body as the first instrument, bare attention as
the first practice, and nothing demanded of the child but presence.
Awareness — the first stage of every path the school walks — begins as
bathing in life, with every sense the child has.

### Tenet 3 — The Laws of the Tree's Wisdom: Roots, Trunk, Canopy

Every tree teaches survival and dignity, and teaches it three-fold:

- **Roots** — ancestral memory, history, the deep strength of a people who
  have endured hard times before and endured them standing.
- **Trunk** — inner resilience, moral fibre, the conscious choice to stand
  tall through the storm.
- **Canopy** — thought, dream, and active care extended outward,
  sheltering others.

The tree's three-fold is the school's own: what was, what is, what is
coming; memory received, strength practised, care given forward. Other
peoples read the same tree — the Norse cosmos stood as Yggdrasil, the
Sakha epic raises the great world tree — for the lesson is written in
every trunk. A child who learns to read a tree this way is reading their
own becoming.

### Tenet 4 — Care as the Highest Prayerful Action

True spirituality is measured not by doctrine but by loving action. A
child's truest prayer is hands in the earth: planting a seedling, clearing
a spring, steadying a peer who struggles. For this reason the school uses
no reward and no punishment to produce participation — an act done for a
prize is obedience, and prayer cannot be obeyed into being. Care is the
school's only currency, and it circulates until no one in the circle is
only a giver or only a receiver.

### Tenet 5 — The Generation of Regeneration

Ukrainian youth are not a lost generation of war. They are the Generation
of Regeneration. By tending their homeland — its soil, its forests, its
springs, its songs — children discover an identity no one can assign and
no war can revoke: *I am part of this land, its guardian, and the builder
of its future.* The school exists so that this discovery can be made by
each child in their own hands, at their own pace, on native soil.

### Tenet 6 — Transformation of Pain: Song, Dance, and the Triumph of Life

The highlands know something the clinics are only now learning: grief that
stays in the body must leave through the body. And the valley is not alone
in knowing it: among the Khanty and Mansi of the Ob, the bear festival
answers awe and death with days and nights of sacred song and story, and
in living memory the Baltic peoples sang their way back to independence.
From the ancestral tradition in which community defied despair together
through night-long vigil, game, and song [for the elders], the school
draws its forms for releasing trauma rather than suppressing it:

- **Singing out the pain.** Polyphonic singing and traditional lament let
  trapped grief and terror leave the body through breath and shared
  resonance — voices holding what no single voice could.
- **Dancing out the trauma.** Rhythmic stomping and circle dances release
  what war froze into the muscles, restoring the body's power and joy in
  movement.
- **Songs that bless.** Sessions close with bright, rising traditional
  songs — old songs of blessing turned toward life, joy, and the child's
  own future.
- **The triumph of life.** As the ancestors defied death around the fire,
  the children learn in their bones what the tradition always taught:
  darkness passes; life endures.

Two hands hold these forms together, always: the tradition-bearers of the
valley, who carry the songs, dances, and games and judge their fitting
use; and the programme's qualified psychological staff, who hold each
child's safety within them. Every practice is entered freely, never
required; grief is invited, never extracted; and a child who only watches
from the circle's edge is honoured exactly where they stand. The tradition
supplies the medicine; the clinical presence guards the dose.

## IV. The Wheel of the Year

The school does not run against the year; it runs with it. Camps can be
held in any season — and the season, not the programme, sets the work.

Planting has its windows: in these mountains, the weeks after the spring
thaw and the weeks before the autumn frost. The school honours them
absolutely. A seedling planted out of season to satisfy a schedule is a
programme serving itself; the land's calendar outranks ours, and teaching
children exactly that is worth more than any planting. When the window
stands open, the cohorts plant. When it is closed, they do the season's
own work — and every season has its own:

- **Spring** — sowing and planting; opening the cleared springs; the beds
  prepared through winter receive their seed.
- **Summer** — tending and watering; the meadow rhythm of the highlands;
  watching seed ripen; the forest at full voice.
- **Autumn** — the harvest of seed for cohorts to come; the second
  planting window; clearing, and composting the year's clearing back into
  the ground.
- **Winter** — the elders' season: craft, story, and song by the fire;
  rest, as the land itself rests; the forest read in snow, where tracks
  reveal the lives that summer hides; tools mended, plans laid, the next
  year's beds made ready.

A cohort that arrives outside the planting windows loses nothing and
learns the most: their gathered seed waits through the cold, and a later
cohort sets it in the ground — inheritance made literal across the turning
year. The Evening of Light takes the colour of its season, for fire in
midwinter says what it cannot say in June. And the three-beat movement
runs at the year's own scale — autumn clears, winter composts, spring
builds — so that the school's one movement now sounds at four scales
together: the forest, the child, the session, the year.

## V. From Philosophy to Programme

This philosophy is not an annex to the Forest School programme; it is the
programme's inner side. The five movements of the child's recovery
(Section I) are what the programme's components serve: the Morning Circle
and forest immersion build safety and reopen the senses; the regeneration
work and the Hack for Hope workshop rebuild purpose and agency; the
Elders' Toloka and the grief-forms of Tenet 6 restore belonging, memory,
and the triumph of life; the Evening of Light and Seeds of Hope mark the
threshold and send each child home carrying proof. The learning
architecture — the concentric rings, the fourfold pathway of Awareness,
Acknowledgment, Attitude, and Action, the three-beat movement of clearing,
composting, and building, the dialogue of the body's senses with simple
measurement, and the sense of place that all of them serve — gives the
philosophy its daily working form, with no reward and no punishment
anywhere in it. The programme document states the architecture,
deliverables, and safeguards in full; two companion surveys — of the
world's kin wisdom, and of kin schools and initiatives worldwide — hold
the lineage; this document states the reason they all exist.

* * *

## VI. What This Is, and Is Not

Spoken publicly, it is what its practice shows: an eco-spiritual
philosophy of the living forest — a manifesto of native land. It founds no
church, ordains no one, and competes with no family's faith; a child of
any belief, or none, walks the same forest and is equally received. What
it keeps of religion is what religion itself once drew from the forest:
reverence, threshold, community, song, and the practice of belonging to
something greater and living. It is a faith measured only in action — and
its first duty is care.
