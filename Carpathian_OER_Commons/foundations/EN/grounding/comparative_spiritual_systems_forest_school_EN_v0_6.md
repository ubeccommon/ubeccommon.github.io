*Companion document to "The Eco-Spiritual Philosophy of the Living
Forest." It surveys the world's spiritual systems and worldviews as kin to
the Forest School's practice. The rule of the survey is stated at its end
and governs throughout: the school honours these traditions by parallel,
never by imitation; the local tradition leads; nothing is transplanted,
and every source is named.*

* * *

## I. The Taproot — The Hutsul and Ukrainian Folk Worldview

The philosophy of the Living Forest grows from this ground and no other.
The Hutsul highlands hold a deep animism: mountains, springs, and trees as
living presences; the high pasture as a lived-with world, never a
wilderness apart. The tradition meets even death in community — the night
vigils in which grief is confronted together through games, laughter, and
song until the fear of death is defeated. Beneath and around it lies the
wider Ukrainian folk layer, where the old earth-faith and Christianity
have long lived braided together: the green holidays when homes and
churches fill with branches and grasses, the midsummer fires and water
rites, songs that bless the field and the year. What the school draws is
everything — this is not a source among sources but the soil in which all
the others are planted, and its carriers, the elders of the valley, hold
the gate on its use. [for the elders]

## II. The Old Ways of Northern and Eastern Europe

Before the surveys cross the oceans, they cross the Baltic — for nowhere
is the old European bond to place buried more shallowly than among the
peoples of the northern and eastern seas, and nowhere in Europe did it
survive longer in the open.

**The Baltic peoples — Lithuania, Latvia, and the old Prussian coast.**
The last peoples of Europe to hold their earth-faith openly: Lithuania
accepted Christianity only at the close of the fourteenth century, and the
old ways never fully left. Sacred groves and holy oaks stood at the heart
of worship — the oak as the thunder-god's tree, felled only at peril — and
the world was sung: the dainas, the vast ocean of Latvian and Lithuanian
folk songs, hold the sun, the seasons, the field-work, and the soul's
passages in hundreds of thousands of four-line verses, a whole cosmology
kept alive in song by grandmothers. And in living memory the singing held:
the Singing Revolution of the Baltic nations faced down an empire with
massed song and won. What the school draws: the deepest European
confirmation of its own practice — that a people's bond to land can be
*kept in song* across centuries of pressure, and that singing together is
not decoration but survival, resistance, and victory. The valley's own
singing and the Baltic dainas are one family.

**The Finnic north — Finland, Karelia, Estonia.** In the Kalevala songs,
the sage does not fight the world into shape; he *sings* it into being,
and power over any thing comes from knowing and singing its origin. The
forest was the church: the Estonian hiis — sacred groves — are tended to
this day, and the old reverence for the bear returned even its bones to
the forest with honour. The bond has passed into modern law as everyman's
right: the freedom of every person to walk, gather, and be at home in the
land, paired with the duty to leave it unharmed. What the school draws:
that to know a being's origin is to be in relationship with it — every
pattern card asking "what is this, and where does it come from" walks the
singer's path — and that belonging to land can be written into a modern
country's law as both right and care.

**The Norse world.** The cosmos itself was a tree: Yggdrasil, roots in
memory and depth, trunk in the living present, crown in the heavens — the
very three-fold the school's children learn to read in every tree. The
land was alive with landvættir, the land spirits: ships approaching home
shores removed their dragon-head prows so as not to affright them —
arrival, even armed arrival, made gentle before the spirits of place. Law
itself was spoken under the open sky, the assembly gathered outdoors on
the land it governed. And wisdom, in the deepest Norse story, was won on
the tree. What the school draws: the tree as the readable cosmos; the
practice of arriving gently on any land; and the circle under open sky as
the oldest parliament. One Norse path the school honours and does not
walk: the solitary night-vigil and the ordeal on the tree belong to the
tradition's adults — the school's children cross every threshold
accompanied.

**The Celts.** The grove — nemeton — was the sanctuary; wells and springs
were doorways of the holy; the year turned on threshold nights when the
veil between worlds thinned. The learned orders trained for as much as
twenty years in the forest itself, wisdom carried wholly in memory and
verse — Europe's original forest school. The trees were even an alphabet:
in ogham, each letter bears a tree's name, so that to learn one's letters
was to learn the forest, and the land was literally the text. Early Irish
law ranked the trees in classes and fined their wounding — nobles of the
wood, protected by statute. What the school draws: the oldest European
precedent for everything it is — a school held in the forest, a curriculum
written in trees, thresholds kept with ceremony, and the law itself
standing guard over the wood.

## III. The Peoples of the Urals, Taiga, Tundra, and Steppe

East of the old European ways, across the Urals and into the taiga and
tundra, live nations whose lands lie within the borders of today's Russian
state. One distinction governs this section absolutely: these are the
traditions of peoples, not of the state that contains them. Most of these
nations were colonised by the same empire Ukraine has known — their
languages suppressed, their shamans persecuted, their ceremonies driven
underground in the Soviet decades — and their survival is kin to Ukraine's
own. The school honours peoples, never empires.

**The Mari.** In the forests of the middle Volga, the Mari keep Europe's
last openly practised grove-faith: the küsoto, sacred groves — hundreds
still active — where the community gathers for prayer ceremonies led by
traditional priests, as it has through every century of pressure. What the
school draws: living proof, inside this very decade, that a grove-faith
can survive empire, church, and modernity in the open — the hiis and the
nemeton not as memory but as neighbour.

**The Khanty and Mansi.** The Ob-Ugrian peoples — the closest linguistic
kin of the Hungarians, Finns, and Estonians — hold the bear in deepest
reverence, and their bear festival meets the gravest of occasions with
songs, stories, and games played through the nights. What the school
draws: startling kinship with the valley's own vigils — the same deep
structure, a community answering death and awe with game and song —
arising independently at the two ends of the Finno-Ugric world.

**The Nenets.** On the tundra of the far north, life moves with the
reindeer as it does in Sápmi: land as partner, the herd as calendar,
belonging carried in movement rather than walls. What the school draws:
the second witness, beside the Sámi, that no landscape is too hard to be
home when it is met as kin.

**The Evenki.** The taiga people of the great forest gave the world its
very word: "shaman" entered every language from the Evenki tongue. Theirs
is the archetypal accompanied crossing — the guided passage between
worlds, made on behalf of the community. What the school draws: the
vocabulary of the oldest human learning-shape comes from this forest; the
school walks the shape's gentle half — accompanied, never alone, never
through ordeal — and knows where the word for it was born.

**The Sakha, and the peoples of Altai, Tuva, and Buryatia.** In the Sakha
epic of Olonkho — recognised among the world's oral masterpieces — the
cosmos stands as Aal Luuk Mas, the great world tree; the ysyakh festival
greets the returning sun. In the Altai mountains and across the steppe,
the eternal sky is revered above sacred peaks, travellers add stones to
the oboo cairns at passes, and in khoomei throat-singing a single voice
sounds the river, the wind, and the mountain at once. What the school
draws: a third cosmos-as-tree beside Yggdrasil and the tenet of roots,
trunk, and canopy; small acts of reverence at the thresholds of travel;
and one more witness that a people's land is held, and healed, in song.

## IV. Turtle Island — The Indigenous Nations of North America

No single worldview covers the hundreds of distinct nations of the land
many of its peoples call Turtle Island; the school listens to teachings
those nations themselves have chosen to share publicly, and names them by
nation.

**The Thanksgiving Address (Haudenosaunee).** The "words before all else":
before any gathering proceeds, greetings and thanks are spoken to the
people, the waters, the plants, the animals, the winds, the sun, moon, and
stars — until the minds of those gathered are one. What the school draws:
its Morning Circle of gratitude stands in unknowing parallel to this
ancient protocol — gratitude not as feeling but as *practice*, spoken in
community, ordering the day before the day begins.

**The Seventh Generation principle (Haudenosaunee).** Decisions weighed by
their effect on the seventh generation to come. What the school draws: the
whole architecture of cohort inheritance — trees planted for children not
yet born, a record handed forward — is this principle at the scale of a
mountainside; children practise seventh-generation thinking with their
hands.

**All My Relations (Lakota: mitákuye oyás'iŋ).** The prayer-word that
names the whole world as family: every being related, the human one
relative among many. What the school draws: its refusal of "wilderness,"
its circle of care in which forest, child, elder, and community are
members of one body — kinship as ecology's true name.

**The Honourable Harvest (as taught by Robin Wall Kimmerer, Potawatomi).**
Never take the first; never take more than half; ask permission; use
everything taken; give a gift in return for what is given. What the school
draws: its ethic for every act of gathering — seed, branch, water, even
knowledge from an elder — taking with asking, using with respect,
returning more than was taken.

## V. Indigenous Kin Across the World

**Aboriginal Australia — Country and the songlines.** Country is kin: a
living relative to be cared for, not a resource. Knowledge of land is held
in songlines — routes walked and sung, where the song *is* the map and the
law. What the school draws: the pattern language of place is a young
cousin of this oldest practice — knowing a land by walking it, naming it,
and singing its knowledge forward so the next traveller can follow.

**Aotearoa — the Māori way.** Whakapapa binds people and the natural world
into one genealogy — the forest is ancestor, not property; kaitiakitanga
names the duty of guardianship that flows from that kinship. In law,
Aotearoa has followed the worldview to its conclusion: the forest Te
Urewera and the Whanganui River are legal persons. What the school draws:
guardianship as inherited duty rather than chosen hobby — the child as
kaitiaki of their own mountainside — and proof that a worldview can
reshape even the law of states.

**The Andes — Pachamama and ayni.** The living earth-mother receives and
gives; ayni, sacred reciprocity, orders human life — help given must
circulate, gifts return as gifts. Sumak kawsay, the good life, means
living well *with*, never at the expense of. What the school draws: ayni
is the toloka's southern twin — the same circulating labour of kinship —
and confirms that the school's economy of care belongs to a worldwide
family of reciprocal economies.

**Sápmi — the Sámi way.** Life moved with the reindeer across tundra and
forest treats land as partner in survival; the joik does not sing *about*
a person, animal, or place — it sings them, presents them, keeps them
present. What the school draws: song as a way of holding beings and places
in memory and presence — a northern kin to the valley's own singing — and
the tundra's lesson that there is no landscape too hard to be home.

**Africa — ubuntu and the sacred groves.** Ubuntu: a person is a person
through other persons — being itself is relational. Across the continent,
sacred groves such as the Yoruba forest of Osun-Osogbo have been protected
for centuries because the sacred and the ecological were never separate
categories. What the school draws: its deepest sentence — care circulating
until no one is only giver or receiver — is ubuntu extended to the land;
and the groves prove that reverence is the oldest and most effective
conservation policy on earth.

## VI. The Asian Ways

**Shinto and shinrin-yoku (Japan).** Nature as sacred space alive with
kami; forest bathing as cleansing of body and spirit, now clinically
studied. What the school draws: sensory immersion as practice — silent
walks, trees met as living beings capable of absorbing pain — the tenet of
Bathing in Life.

**Daoism (China).** The living flow of the world; naturalness; wu-wei,
action through non-striving; water and softness as true strength. What the
school draws: its manner — patient observation, adaptation to rhythm, the
forest and the child each allowed to work at their own pace; the
gardener's restraint as a spiritual discipline.

**Buddhism.** Mindfulness as bare attention; interbeing — the teaching
that nothing exists separately, the tree in the paper, the cloud in the
tree; impermanence held without despair. What the school draws: the
quality of attention in its observation practices, and the composting
movement's deepest logic — that what passes feeds what comes, and letting
go is a form of care.

**The Vedic and village traditions of India.** Sacred groves (devrai and
their many regional names) protected as the gods' own; ahimsa, non-harm,
as the first ethic; and in living memory the Chipko movement, in which
village women embraced trees with their bodies to stop the axes. What the
school draws: the knowledge that children defending a forest stand in a
long line of ordinary people whose care became protection — reverence as
resistance, embodied.

## VII. The Abrahamic Ways

**Christianity — and first the churches of this valley.** The Orthodox and
Greek-Catholic traditions that hold the Carpathians carry a deep theology
of creation: the world as icon of its maker, blessed with water and branch
through the year's feasts; the hesychast practice of stillness and
watchfulness of heart; and in the wider church, from the saint of Assisi
who called the creatures brother and sister to the encyclical Laudato Si'
on care for our common home, an unbroken green thread. What the school
draws: confidence that its philosophy stands *beside* the faith of the
families it serves, not against it — reverence for creation is the
church's own inheritance, and the school's silence, gratitude, and care
are practices a believing family recognises as friendly ground.

**Judaism.** Bal tashchit — the command not to destroy wantonly, rooted in
the Torah's protection of fruit trees even in war; shmita, the sabbath of
the land, a rest every seventh year; Tu BiShvat, the new year of the
trees, when planting is celebration. What the school draws: rhythm as
sacred law — even the land must rest — and the oldest legal protection of
trees in wartime, written three thousand years before this war.

**Islam.** The human as khalifa — trustee, not owner, of the earth; mizan,
the balance set in creation that must not be transgressed; the teaching
that one who plants a tree from which any creature eats has given charity.
What the school draws: stewardship as trusteeship — the land held in care
for its true owner and for those to come — and the planting of a tree as
an act of generosity that outlives the planter.

## VIII. The Modern Convergence

**Biophilia and eco-spirituality.** The scientific hypothesis that humans
carry an innate need for connection with living systems, and the growing
practice of spirituality expressed through care rather than dogma. What
the school draws: its plainest creed — a practical faith of hands in the
soil — now speakable in the language of research as well as reverence.

**Deep ecology.** The philosophical position that the living world has
worth in itself, not only as human resource, and that the self, fully
understood, extends into the web of relations that sustain it. What the
school draws: the intellectual articulation of what the traditions above
have always practised — and a bridge language for partners who arrive
through philosophy rather than faith.

**The rights of nature.** From Aotearoa's forest-person to Ecuador's
constitution, the world's law has begun to say what the old worldviews
always said: the living world is a subject, not only an object. What the
school draws: evidence, for funders and sceptics, that the worldview it
practises with children is not nostalgia — it is where the world is going.

## IX. The Common Patterns

Read together, the traditions do not blur into sameness; they show a
recurring deep structure, and the school is built on it:

- **Kinship, not wilderness.** Everywhere the oldest worldviews refuse the
  cut between human and nature: relations, ancestors, Country, kami,
  creation — never "environment."
- **Reciprocity as law.** Ayni, toloka, the Honourable Harvest, ubuntu,
  khalifa's trust: taking obliges giving; care must circulate or the world
  sickens.
- **Gratitude as protocol.** Not a mood but a practice with a form — words
  before all else, grace, blessing, the morning circle.
- **Land as teacher and text.** Songlines, pattern languages, the ogham
  letters named for trees, the tree's three laws: the ground itself is the
  curriculum, and sometimes the alphabet.
- **Song and ceremony where words fail.** Grief, threshold, and triumph
  are carried in the body — vigil, joik, lament, dance — in every
  tradition that has survived hard history.
- **Long time.** Seventh generations, shmita cycles, cathedral groves: the
  traditions think in tree-time, and teach it.
- **The accompanied crossing.** Into the forest, the tundra, the mountain
  — with a guide, and back with a gift. The oldest shape of learning on
  earth.

That this structure recurs from the Carpathians to the Baltic and across
the Urals, from Turtle Island to Aotearoa, is the strongest evidence the
school's philosophy could ask for: it is not inventing a way of being; it
is rejoining one — humanity's oldest and most widely shared.

concept: each system, its core philosophy, and what the Forest School
draws from it.

  grief met in community through vigil, game, and song; earth-faith and
  Christianity braided. Drawn: the taproot — grief-forms, singing, dance,
  the elders' authority over all use. [for the elders]

  survival, resistance, victory.

  being; power through knowing origins; hiis groves; everyman's right.

## XI. The Ethic of Borrowing

One rule governs the school's relationship to every tradition in this
survey, and it is absolute:

The school honours by parallel, never by imitation. It does not perform
the ceremonies of other peoples, does not adopt their sacred forms, and
does not blend their practices into an unrooted mixture. It practises its
*own* place's forms — Hutsul and Ukrainian, under the elders' authority —
and recognises, with gratitude and by name, the kin traditions of the
world that have walked the same deep structure. Where a teaching from
elsewhere informs the school's understanding, it is credited to its
people; where a practice belongs to a people, it stays with them. The
survey above is a family album, not a catalogue of parts. The school's
answer to the world's wisdom is not to borrow its songs but to sing, more
faithfully, its own.
