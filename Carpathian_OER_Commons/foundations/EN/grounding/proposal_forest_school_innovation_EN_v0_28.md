## Thesis

The thesis of Forest School is three-fold.

The first fold is near, and it is not a metaphor. A war is being fought in
this country, and children carry it. Forest School leads war-affected
children and youth into a literal Carpathian forest so that learning —
discovered by them, never delivered to them — leads them out of the
figurative one: the forest of war, loss, and disorientation. In tending
the land they are tended by it; care circulates through children, mentors,
elders, community, and forest until no one in the circle is only a giver
or only a receiver. This is the school's first reason, and it would be
reason enough.

The second fold is deep, and it is why the school would need to exist even
in peace. The war did not create the wound the school answers; it tore the
cover off it. Beneath the visible war lies a severance that is worldwide
and wears a different face in every place: person cut from land, person
from person, generation from generation, values from action. Open war is
its most violent face — not its only one. The school's deeper reason is
spiritual in the plainest sense of the word: it exists to practise
reconnection — of child to ground, of person to person, of the present to
its past and its future — and that need ends at no border and no
ceasefire.

The third fold is forward, and it is the necessary one, because a school
that existed only against a wound would end when the wound closed, and the
first two folds alone would only mourn. Forest School exists for what it
is growing: a generation that knows in its hands how to belong to a place,
how to regenerate what is broken, how to receive from elders and hand on
to those who come after — and a freely licensed model that other places
can take up and fill with their own life. The wound explains the school's
beginning; the gift explains its future. In many paths of the spirit the
fold is three — what was, what is, what is coming — and it is the third
that turns grief into work and work into hope.

The school orders its own life by the same conviction. Its convictions are
lived in the open and pressed on no one: every pattern is named in the
child's own words, and every family's faith walks with them unasked. One
standing rule holds everyone — child, mentor, elder, guest — and no one
stands above another. No place at the table is earned; every need is
carried in common, and the only currency that circulates is care. The
school practises this at the scale of a table and a working day. Nothing
larger is claimed for it.

The three folds are one movement — named in clear thought, transformed
through the feeling heart, grown by willing hands. The hand that heals a
forest and the hand that heals a child are the same hand. What remains
when a session ends — trees in the ground, a growing record of the place's
own patterns, bonds between generations — is peace built as
infrastructure, not conducted as process. An old Ukrainian proverb holds
the whole: *Наука в ліс не веде, а з лісу виводить* — learning does not
lead into the forest, it leads out of it.

## Mission Statement

Forest School exists so that children and land can heal and grow —
together, each through the other.

We do not heal anyone, and we do not teach in the old sense. We prepare
ground, keep rhythm, and accompany. The healing is done by the forest, the
circle, and the child, at the child's own pace; the learning is done by
the learner. Our work is the gardener's work: tending the conditions in
which growth does what only growth can do.

We bring children and youth from across the country into living
relationship with a Carpathian forest, its community, and its elders, so
that in tending what is wounded they are tended themselves. Through
discovery rather than instruction, rhythm rather than pressure, and real
work with real consequence, we tend the conditions in which each child
grows the capacities no war can take: attention, belonging, competence,
care, and hope.

We regenerate a forest, sowing and planting native trees into ground each
cohort clears, composts, and feeds. We keep a record of the place's own
patterns, written by children and elders together, and hand it on. We work
as the mountains have always worked — together, across generations, with
no place at the table earned and every need carried in common.

And we build all of this in the open: an ethos every family can read
before choosing it, a method any community can take up freely, and a
prototype co-created with this place so that other places, in other
contexts and cultures, can grow their own form of the healing inside the
same shape.

## Executive Summary

Forest School is a residential programme for children and youth at a
Hutsul highland site in the Ukrainian Carpathians, combining psychosocial
rehabilitation, ecological education, values-based reflection, and
youth-led social innovation. Participants come from across the country;
every session runs with qualified psychological and medical presence, a
defined mentoring ratio, and full meal provision. The programme applies
the approach of Soil and Peace, a community regeneration practice: peace
grown as infrastructure — capacities, relationships, and living systems
that remain — rather than negotiated as process. It answers the documented
values-action gap of environmental education by never separating the two:
action is immediate, tangible, and recognised, and the learning is the
acting.

The learning method is a pattern language of place, drawn from the Erdpuls
open educational resource collection of that name. Children work as
discoverers: attention moves outward through concentric rings — body,
homestead, garden and forest edge, village, valley — through a four-stage
pathway of Awareness, Acknowledgment, Attitude, and Action, and every
discovery is recorded on safeguarding-adapted pattern cards that each
cohort inherits from the last and extends. Beneath the method runs the
three-beat movement of all regeneration — clearing, composting, building —
which the forest teaches literally and the child is never made to perform.
The body is the first instrument and simple measurement the second,
admitted by a strict filter; the model is designed and documented in that
collection, and Forest School carries it into a second landscape and
language community.

In 2026 the programme carries seven components: a daily Morning Circle of
gratitude and hope; the Forest of Peace regeneration strand, in which
children work the soil, clear and compost, and sow, raise, and plant
native trees; the "Hack for Hope" youth social innovation workshop, in
which teams design responses to needs they have themselves observed; the
Elders' Toloka, one or more intergenerational co-creation sessions in
which elders of the community and region work beside the children and
learning runs in every direction; the song and dance forms of the highland
tradition [for the elders], freely entered and clinically held, through
which grief leaves the body in community; the closing Evening of Light;
and the Seeds of Hope recognition, which sends every child home seen and
carrying a living seed of the work.

The pedagogy is grounded, at method level only, in Waldorf and Goethean
practice — rhythm, head-heart-hands, observation before explanation — held
within two firm boundaries: no doctrine is taught, and all cultural
content is Hutsul and Ukrainian. Child protection is a non-optional
condition throughout: no child is named, depicted, dated, counted, or
placed in any published material, and all visiting adults work under the
programme's safeguarding supervision. Youth empowerment and capacity
building run through the whole as a trajectory: participant to peer mentor
to co-facilitator across returning seasons, with the workshop roles and
leadership steps stated as deliverables. Beneath every method lies the
capacity they all serve — the sense of place, the practised ability to
belong to real ground, held in the old threshold shape of separation,
accompanied crossing, and return, without ordeal, with its ethos in the
open and doctrine pressed on no one. Outcomes are read at the level of the
standing practice — the forest's recovery, the thickening pattern record,
the returning elders, and the children's own return across seasons — never
through any individual child.

## 1. What the Programme Is

Forest School is a residential programme for children and youth, delivered
at a Hutsul highland site in the Ukrainian Carpathians. It combines
psychosocial rehabilitation, ecological education, values-based
reflection, and youth-led social innovation.

Participants come from across the country. The programme runs with
qualified psychological and medical presence, a defined mentoring ratio,
full meal provision, and a standing session format.

The programme applies the approach of Soil and Peace, a community
regeneration practice: the conviction that the hand that heals soil and
the hand that heals what lies between people is the same hand, and that
peace is not a process to be negotiated but an infrastructure to be grown
— capacities, relationships, and living systems that hold when events do
not. A peace process ends when the signing ends. Peace infrastructure — a
forest that children planted together, a circle of care they learned to
stand inside, a pattern language they made their own — remains, and
carries. Forest School is built as peace infrastructure.

The child's recovery orders the work in five movements: safety first —
trust, groundedness, and personal boundary restored before anything else
begins; the senses reopened, from war's numbness back to soil, scent,
stillness, and joy; purpose and identity redefined — the child's
self-perception moving from "victim of war" to creator, defender, and
steward of life on their native land, so that Ukrainian youth grow not as
a lost generation of war but as the Generation of Regeneration; faith in
light and goodness restored through belonging to something larger and
living; and the flourishing forest itself as living proof — telling each
child a truth no words could carry, that they have the power to nurture,
to heal, and to give life back to the world.

The forest is not the backdrop of the programme and not its object. It is
a participant and a caregiver in its own right. The western habit of mind
separates the human from a so-called wilderness; the peoples who have
lived longest with their land — here, the Hutsuls of these highlands — did
not make that cut [for the elders]. There is no wilderness in this valley.
There is land in relationship: pasture, forest, garden, homestead, each
tended and each tending back. The programme stands inside that older
understanding. The children do not only learn in the forest and work on
the forest — the forest works on them. Its quiet, its scale, its
indifference to the war, its steady green insistence on continuing: these
are the forest's side of the care.

## 2. What Innovation Means Here

An old Ukrainian proverb anchors the whole approach:

*Наука в ліс не веде, а з лісу виводить.*
*Learning does not lead into the forest — it leads out of it.*

The forest of the proverb is the dark one: being lost, not knowing, fear.
The programme takes the proverb at its word by an apparent contradiction —
it leads children into the literal forest so that learning can lead them
out of the figurative one. Among the trees, in work that has rhythm,
meaning, and visible result, the forest of war — disorientation, loss,
isolation — begins to loosen its hold. The child who learns the forest is
no longer lost in it; the child who tends the forest is no longer only
what the war made of them. The way into the trees is the way out of the
dark.

Within that frame, innovation is not a technology and not a novelty. It is
a change in the direction of agency.

The change answers a documented failure of conventional environmental
education: the values-action gap. Large majorities of young Europeans say
they care about the living world; far fewer carry that care into daily
action, because knowing about a system is not the same as belonging to
one. The gap closes when action is immediate, tangible, and recognised — a
tree planted with one's own hands, a pattern discovered and named, a
project presented to a real community. Forest School is built so that
values and action are never separated in the first place: the child does
not first learn and later act; the learning is the acting.

Much psychosocial support for war-affected children is, of necessity,
delivered FOR them: care arrives, and they receive it. That work is
necessary and it continues here. The innovation of Forest School 2026 is
the deliberate second movement: the children are brought into work done
WITH them — and then into work they lead themselves, done for the land and
for others.

Innovation, in this context, is therefore defined as:

- **Circulation of care.** A child who arrives as a recipient of
  protection becomes a giver of care — to a forest, to a community idea,
  to another person's hope — and discovers, in giving it, that care was
  flowing toward them the whole time: from the mentors, from the peer
  group, from the land itself. The direction of care does not simply
  reverse; it becomes a circle. The community cares for the child, the
  child cares for the forest, the forest cares for the child, the child
  returns to care for the community. A person is a person through other
  persons, as the Nguni proverb of ubuntu has it — and, in this valley,
  through the land those persons belong to, which is our extension, not
  the proverb's. Neither caregiver nor cared-for is a fixed role; everyone
  and everything in the circle is both.
- **Integration, not addition.** Healing, ecological work, ethical
  reflection, and creative problem-solving are not four parallel activity
  blocks. They are one process, done by the same hand.
- **Rootedness in an indigenous form.** The working method is *toloka*,
  shared work known across these mountains and their neighbours: people
  doing together a piece of work none could do alone. The hackathon, the
  planting, the closing festival are all *toloka* in youth form. The
  innovation is not imported; it joins a practice the mountains already
  hold.
- **Discovery, not delivery.** The programme's learning method is the
  pattern language of place: children are not taught facts about the
  forest, they discover its patterns — and name them in their own words. A
  pattern language the children have not themselves discovered is not
  theirs; it is someone else's, imposed. The proverb holds here too:
  learning that is handed over leaves a child where it found them;
  learning a child makes their own is what leads out of the forest.
- **Empowerment as trajectory, not event.** Youth empowerment and capacity
  building are integral to the programme, and they are built as a path
  rather than a workshop: a child participates; a returning youth mentors
  younger peers; an older youth co-leads a team, a circle, a planting.
  Each return across seasons is a step from being held toward holding. The
  programme's aim is that its future facilitators are already inside it,
  growing.
- **Regeneration, not repair.** The programme does not aim to return
  children or land to a pre-war state. It aims to grow capacities —
  resilience, empathy, initiative, ecological literacy — that did not need
  to exist before and now do.

A question runs beneath these markers: are the children being formed as
caregivers, or as stewards of the future? The programme holds that the
question answers itself once the separation is refused. A steward manages
something entrusted — the word still stands slightly outside and above
what it tends. A caregiver stands inside a relationship, in the present
tense, and is cared for in return. The children become caregivers now,
within a living circle of care — and it is precisely this, not any
management of the future, that the future will inherit. The future is not
stewarded from a distance. It is grown, the way a forest is grown: by
tending the present relationship well and trusting what it seeds.

## 3. The Method — A Pattern Language of Place

The programme's learning architecture is drawn from the Erdpuls open
educational resource collection *Pattern Language of Place* and its
Pattern Discovery Toolkit. Three structures from that collection carry the
whole:

### 3.1 Patterns, Discovered Not Taught

Following Christopher Alexander, a pattern is a relationship that works —
between a context, a tension, and a resolution observed to hold,
repeatedly, over time. Every place has a pattern language; the task is not
to invent it but to notice it. At Forest School the children work as
pattern-discoverers: observing before explaining, describing what the
forest shows them before being told what it means, and naming what they
find in their own words. The method is phenomenological in the Goethean
sense — *zarte Empirie*, delicate empiricism — and follows a three-step
discipline drawn from Goethe: *collect, order, form*. First the glance at
the whole, which produces wonder; then the patient gathering of phenomena,
with judgement held back; then the ordering, which is never forced but
allowed to emerge from the things themselves; and only last the forming of
a pattern — a lawfulness read from the phenomena, tested against further
observation. The body is the first instrument; simple measurement (soil,
water, species) is the second, and the dialogue between the two is where
the learning happens.

Instruments are admitted by a filter, not by default: a tool enters the
children's work only where it serves the encounter rather than replacing
it — understandable and repairable, working with the day's rhythms rather
than against them, appropriate to the place, connecting the children to
the ground and to each other rather than to a screen, and generating no
data that places any child or the site. The body always comes first;
nothing with a battery precedes a hand in the soil.

Each discovered pattern is recorded on a pattern card — the tension it
resolves, what was observed, how it connects to other patterns, what is
not yet known. The cards accumulate across cohorts. Session by session,
year by year, the children assemble the forest's own pattern language — a
living document no single group could write alone, and each group inherits
and extends. This is the *toloka* at the scale of knowledge.

The card format is adapted for safeguarding: cards carry no child's name
and no coordinates. Discoveries are attributed to the cohort, and place is
recorded at the level of the ring. The record belongs to the group and to
the forest, not to any identifiable child.

### 3.2 The Concentric Rings

Attention moves outward from the centre, never the reverse — no maps or
abstractions before the ground itself. Adapted to this site, the rings
are:

- **Ring 0 — the body.** Embodied perception as the calibrating
  instrument. Bare attention: what does this place show you now?
- **Ring 1 — the homestead.** The buildings, materials, and micro-climates
  of the camp site itself.
- **Ring 2 — the garden and forest edge.** Where the closely tended and
  the self-willed land meet — not a boundary between human and wilderness,
  for there is none here, but a gradient of relationship; the regeneration
  strand works here.
- **Ring 3 — the village.** The settlement as a pattern-rich environment:
  craft, heritage, community forms.
- **Ring 4 — the valley.** Watershed, pasture, geology — the *polonyna*
  landscape as the widest frame of belonging.

Each ring is also a sensory register: at Ring 0 every channel is open —
touch, smell, warmth, sound; at Ring 4, vision leads. The day's forms
follow this grammar deliberately — the Morning Circle and shared work are
gathering forms, seated in a round, hands in common material; solitary
observation disperses; the Evening of Light gathers again around a single
point of light.

### 3.3 The 4A-Pathway

Every learning sequence follows four stages, and the whole camp follows
them as one arc:

- **Awareness** — *what is here?* The direct sensory encounter, protected
  from premature explanation. The forest work and the day's first
  encounters live here.
- **Acknowledgment** — *how does this connect to me?* Pattern naming; the
  recognition that what was observed matters — personally, locally,
  ecologically.
- **Attitude** — *what does this mean for how I want to live?* Values
  reflection; the questions of kindness, responsibility, and hope the
  circle carries.
- **Action** — *what will I do because of this?* The Hack for Hope
  projects, the trees planted, the seed carried home.

The pathway spirals rather than ends: a child who reached Awareness with a
patch of soil one season may return the next and move into Acknowledgment
with the same ground. The programme is built for that return — each
cohort's pattern cards and planted trees are the standing invitation.

The proverb and the pathway are one movement. Awareness leads the child
into the forest; Action leads them out of it — changed, carrying
something, leaving something growing behind.

### 3.4 The Three Movements — Clearing, Composting, Building

Soil and Peace works in a three-beat movement, and the forest teaches it
without being asked: *clearing* — *composting* — *building*. What is
cleared; what is let to rot down and feed the next thing; what is raised.

In the forest, the movement is literal. The children leave standing and
fallen deadwood as habitat and clear only what chokes the young growth;
nothing cleared is discarded — it is composted, returned to the soil it
came from, where it becomes the fertility the seedlings will stand in; and
into that fed ground the new trees are planted. The waste of the old
forest is the food of the new one. There is no away.

In the child, the same movement runs — and it is never named to the
children, never assigned, never forced. The Morning Circle quietly makes
room for clearing: what a child sets down there is their own to set down.
Reflection and story let hard things break down slowly into something that
can feed rather than poison. And the building — the planting, the
projects, the seed carried home — rises out of that ground. The programme
does not perform this parallel at the children; it trusts the forest to
teach it, at each child's own pace, through the hands.

The camp arc follows the same beat: arrival and settling as clearing; the
middle days of work, story, and rest as composting; the closing days —
Hack for Hope, the Evening of Light, the Seeds of Hope — as building. One
movement, four scales: the forest, the child, the session, and the year
itself — autumn clears, winter composts, spring builds.

### 3.5 Technology — the Second Instrument

The programme refuses the old opposition of technology and nature. A
thermometer in the soil and a hand in the soil are not rivals; they are
two instruments asking the same question, and the gap between their
answers is where the learning lives. The hand says: sheltered here,
exposed there. The sensor says: three degrees warmer by the east wall.
Neither reading replaces the other — the dialogue between them is a more
complete way of knowing a place than either alone, and it teaches the
children something larger than ecology: that measurement and reverence can
hold the same object at the same time.

Rightly held, technology extends attention rather than replacing it. A
simple measurement kept across the season — soil warmth, moisture, the
returning species — watches the young trees through the weeks when no
cohort is present, and the readings join the pattern record beside the
children's own observations. Technology here does what the
cohort-inheritance already does: it carries attention across time, so that
each group receives not only the last group's trees and cards but its
measurements, and the forest's recovery becomes legible across years.

At its best, human making is the forest's own intelligence continued
through human hands — the leaf preceded the solar panel, the root web
preceded the network. The children meet technology not as the opposite of
the living world but as its apprentice. The filter in Section 3.1 is this
same conviction stated as discipline: a tool is admitted where it serves
life and the encounter, refused where it displaces them, and no tool
generates data that places a child or the site.

### 3.6 Sense of Place — the Oldest Curriculum

Beneath every method in this section lies the capacity they all serve: the
sense of place — the lived, embodied knowledge of belonging to a
particular ground. Rings, patterns, pathway, measurement: each is a way of
practising attachment to somewhere real. This is the oldest curriculum
there is. Across cultures and ages, people have crossed out of the settled
world into the forest, the mountain, the far pasture — always accompanied,
by a guide, an elder, a healer — and returned changed, carrying something
the community needed. The shape is constant: separation, threshold,
return.

Forest School keeps the shape and holds it gently. The children leave home
and cross into a held time among the trees; the mentors and elders are the
accompaniment the old pattern always required; the Evening of Light marks
the threshold; and every child returns carrying something — a pattern seen
with their own eyes, a seed for their own ground, an intention spoken in
the circle. The return is the point: the forest is entered so that home
can be re-entered differently.

Two boundaries hold the old shape safe for these children. First, the
shape transfers; the ordeal does not. The programme uses no isolation, no
deprivation, no engineered crisis — the practices some traditions used to
force the threshold. Children in psychosocial recovery need the opposite:
safety, accompaniment, rhythm, and a forest allowed to work at each
child's own pace. Second, the ethos in the open — doctrine to no one. The
programme is not neutral and does not pretend to be. It holds convictions
and lives them where they can be seen: that the land is alive with
relationship; that care circulates until no one is only giver or only
receiver; that attention ripens into attachment, attachment into care;
that what is broken is not repaired but regenerated. These convictions
belong to no one person and are imported from nowhere: they are co-created
— by the household, the mentors, the elders, the children, and the place
itself — and held in common, growing as the circle that holds them grows.
And because the convictions are co-created rather than transplanted, the
programme works as a prototype: what can travel to other contexts and
cultures is the architecture — the rings, the pathway, the movements, the
safeguards, the openness — never the content. Each new place that takes up
the pattern fills it with its own memory, its own elders, its own names
for things, and co-creates its own way of being inside the same shape.
Families who choose the programme are choosing this way of being, and
their choice is made real by openness: the ethos is stated plainly in what
parents read before consenting, never discovered afterwards. What the
programme will not do is turn the ethos into instruction — the convictions
are enacted, never catechised; no metaphysical claim is taught to a child
as truth; and the programme stands beside, never in place of, each
family's own faith. Each child's belief, or none, walks with them unasked.
The ceremonies, the reverence, the thresholds — these are human capacities
older than any doctrine, and here they remain capacities: practised by
all, imposed on none.

This curriculum carries a further gift, offered without ever being named
to the children: the sense of place is a capacity, not only a bond to one
ground. A child who has practised belonging — who has learned how
attention becomes attachment and attachment becomes care — carries that
practice to whatever ground their life reaches next. War can take a place.
The capacity to belong, once grown, is harder to take.

## 4. The 2026 Components

### 4.1 Morning Circle of Gratitude and Hope

Each programme day begins with a twenty-minute reflective practice focused
on emotional grounding and resilience.

Activities include:

- gratitude reflection
- short stories of courage and compassion
- guided reflection on kindness, responsibility, and hope
- moments of silence, with optional individual prayer
- sharing intentions for the day

The circle is rhythmic and predictable by design: the same form, the same
time, every day. For children whose recent experience has been
unpredictability, the reliability of the form is itself part of the
therapy. The practice rests on universal humanitarian and spiritual values
— empathy, solidarity, dignity, hope, mutual support — and is
non-confessional throughout.

### 4.2 Forest of Peace — the Regeneration Strand (new)

The Forest of Peace is literal. Across the programme, children take part
in hands-on forest regeneration on the programme site:

- beginning with the soil itself — hands in the ground before any tool,
  the soil observed, smelled, questioned, and fed, because a forest is
  grown from below and the ground is what holds
- leaving standing and fallen deadwood as habitat, clearing only what
  chokes the young growth, and composting what is cleared back into the
  soil — nothing cleared is thrown away
- collecting and sowing seed of native Carpathian species
- raising and planting seedlings
- tending young trees planted by earlier groups
- learning the forest as a living system — soil, water, fungi, succession
  — through direct work rather than lecture
- discovering and recording the forest's patterns on pattern cards
  (Section 3.1) — each cohort reading the cards of the last, testing them
  against the season, and adding its own
- keeping one living being through the session — each child chooses a tree
  or plant near the camp and returns to it daily for a few quiet minutes:
  not naming, not classifying, simply perceiving how it changes. Returning
  children resume their own being from the season before. Nothing is
  demanded of the child in these minutes but attention; what grows from
  long attention — attachment, and from attachment the wish to care — is
  the method's fruit, never its assignment.

The strand is designed by regenerative principles the forest itself
demonstrates: observe and interact before intervening; prefer small and
slow solutions on the seedling's timescale; produce no waste, since
everything cleared feeds the ground; value diversity, planting the native
mix rather than the single species; and work the edges, since the meeting
of tended and self-willed land is where life is richest.

The work follows the mountain calendar, never the programme's convenience.
Planting is confined to its true windows — after the spring thaw and
before the autumn frost — and is never forced out of season to satisfy a
schedule. Sessions outside the windows do the season's own regeneration
work: sowing in beds, tending and watering, clearing and composting,
collecting seed for cohorts to come, preparing ground, and in winter
reading the forest in snow, mending tools, and keeping the craft and story
work of the elders' season. A cohort that gathers seed the cold holds
until a later cohort plants it makes the inheritance literal across the
turning year.

Each cohort inherits the work of the ones before it and leaves work for
the ones after — trees in the ground and cards in the record. No child
sees the forest finished; every child sees it further along. This is
deliberate: it teaches that recovery — of a forest, of a country — is
longer than any one person's effort and still depends on each person's
effort.

The spiritual dimension is carried in the work itself, not in instruction:
reverence practised as care for a living thing, patience practised on a
seedling's timescale, hope practised as planting what one will not
personally harvest. And the care runs both ways. While the children heal
the forest, the forest heals the children — this is the conviction the
strand is built on. The regeneration of the land and the regeneration of
the child are one process observed from two sides.

Here the anchoring proverb is lived rather than quoted: the children go
into the forest as learners and the learning leads them out — out of
helplessness, out of the war's version of who they are — carrying
knowledge the forest gave them and leaving the forest more alive than they
found it.

### 4.3 "Hack for Hope" — Youth Social Innovation

In the closing days of each session, participants work in teams to develop
creative responses to real needs: support for displaced families,
protection of local ecosystems, stronger and kinder communities, youth-led
recovery.

Ideas take form as handmade prototypes, posters, storytelling, simple
digital concepts, and shared design sessions. The method is *toloka* at
the scale of an idea: no team's project can be built by one member alone.

The hackathon is the Action stage of the 4A-Pathway made visible. Teams
begin from patterns they have themselves discovered during the session — a
tension noticed in the forest, the village, or their own communities — and
design toward its resolution. The solutions are grown from what was
observed, not imported from elsewhere; this is what makes them the
children's own.

The workshop is also where capacity building is most explicit. Working in
teams under light facilitation, participants practise capacities that
outlast any single idea: cooperative work and shared decision-making,
design from observation, giving form to a thought (prototype, poster,
story), presenting to a community, and receiving response. Older youth
take defined roles — team facilitation, time-keeping, presentation
coaching — and returning youth step into co-leadership of the workshop
itself, so that the programme's leadership pipeline runs through its own
participants.

The forest is the workshop's design teacher. Before reaching for a
solution, teams are invited to ask how the forest already solves the
problem — how it stores, distributes, signals, repairs, endures — and to
design from that answer. And every project, digital or handmade, is tested
against a design ethic the teams apply themselves: does it connect people
or isolate them; can the people it serves understand and repair it; does
it work with the place and its rhythms or against them; does it give more
than it takes. The youth thereby practise not only making, but judging
what is worth making — the capacity their generation will need most.

### 4.4 The Elders' Toloka — Intergenerational Co-Creation

In one or two sessions each year — more, as the relationship grows —
elders of the community and the wider region are invited to join the
programme, not as speakers but as co-workers. The form is the oldest one
the mountains have: people of different ages doing a piece of work
together that none could do alone.

The elders and the children plant, tend, and make side by side. Wisdom is
not delivered from a podium; it surfaces in the work — the name of a
plant, the reason a tool is held this way, how a slope was mown before
machines, what this forest looked like sixty years ago and what stood
where the young trees now go. The elder memory is treated as what it is:
living data about the place, reaching further back than any instrument.

The learning runs in every direction:

- **Children learn from elders** — land knowledge, craft, songs, the names
  things carry in this valley, and the steadying fact of lives that have
  already passed through hard times and continued.
- **Elders learn from children** — the pattern cards, the simple
  measurements, fresh eyes on ground they know so well that habit has made
  parts of it invisible; and the plain sight of children planting trees on
  their mountainside, which is its own answer to a hard year.
- **Elders learn from each other, and children from children** — the
  shared work puts people beside neighbours and peers they might never
  otherwise work with, and the exchange inside each generation is as real
  as the exchange between them.

The pattern record gains a dimension of time. Elders can confirm, contest,
or extend the children's discovered patterns — this corner was always wet;
that wind means rain by evening; we called this by another name. Patterns,
it turns out, are not only seasonal but generational, and the record
becomes a conversation across decades.

Through these sessions the 4A-Pathway reaches into the past as well as the
ground. The children move through the same four stages toward the living
culture of the highlands that they move through toward the forest:
Awareness — meeting a song, a tool, a dialect word, a way of mowing,
directly and without explanation; Acknowledgment — the recognition that
this is not folklore but inheritance, the way one's own people answered
this land; Attitude — the question of what, of all this, I want to carry;
Action — practising a craft, singing the song forward, and, where the
elders consent, recording what they share so it joins the record.
Technology serves here as the culture's respectful scribe, never its
substitute: a recording made with consent keeps a voice; only the
practising keeps the practice. In this way the programme is not ignorant
of the past and not ignorant of the tools of the present — it sets them to
work for each other.

The elders' sessions are peace infrastructure in the most direct sense:
bonds between generations, and between children from across the country
and the people of this valley, that continue after the session ends. And
they complete the circle of care — the community that cares for the
children is cared for in return, seen, asked, and needed.

### 4.5 Song, Dance, and the Triumph of Life

From the highlands' own tradition — in which community meets grief and awe
together, through night vigils of song, story, and game [for the elders] —
the programme carries forms for releasing what war leaves in the body:

- **Singing out the pain.** Polyphonic singing and traditional song let
  trapped grief leave the body through breath and shared resonance — many
  voices holding what no single voice could.
- **Dancing out the trauma.** Rhythmic stomping and circle dances may
  loosen what fear held in the body, and give back the body's power and
  joy in movement.
- **Songs that bless.** Sessions close with bright, rising traditional
  songs turned toward life and the child's own future.

Two hands hold these forms together, always: the tradition-bearers of the
valley, who carry the songs, dances, and games and judge their fitting
use; and the programme's qualified psychological staff, who hold each
child's safety within them. Every practice is entered freely, never
required; grief is invited, never extracted; and a child who only watches
from the circle's edge is honoured exactly where they stand. The tradition
supplies the medicine; the clinical presence guards the dose. The valley
is not alone in this knowledge: kin traditions from the Finno-Ugric north
to the Baltic have met hard history the same way — in community, in the
body, in song.

### 4.6 Evening of Light

Each session closes with a symbolic evening: teams present their ideas,
reflections and gratitude are shared, and a candlelight moment holds hope
for Ukraine. The evening is a festival in the old sense — a community
marking a threshold together — and it answers, at the scale of the whole
session, the form the Morning Circle opens each day.

### 4.7 Seeds of Hope — Recognition

Every participant is recognised at the close of the programme:

- a personalised certificate of participation
- a small symbolic token of the place
- a literal seed or seedling to carry home — the Forest of Peace extended
  into the child's own ground

Recognition names what each child carried into the common work.
Recognition is universal and descriptive rather than ranked: every child
is seen for something true, and no child is placed above another. It
follows the work rather than purchasing it — nothing at Forest School is
done for a reward or under a threat, and the recognition is never
announced in advance as the price of participation.

## 5. Pedagogical Grounding

Several elements of the programme run structurally parallel to Waldorf /
anthroposophically-informed pedagogy, and the 2026 draft makes that
grounding explicit at method level:

- **Rhythm as therapy.** The fixed daily arc — circle, work, meal, work,
  rest — with the closing festival marking the session's threshold,
  mirrors the Waldorf principle that a breathing rhythm of concentration
  and release is itself stabilising for the child. For war-affected
  children this is not aesthetic preference; it is trauma-informed
  structure.
- **Head, heart, hands.** Every component engages thinking (design,
  ecology), feeling (gratitude, story, festival), and willing (planting,
  building, making). Nothing is taught that is not also done.
- **Reverence and gratitude as moods, not lessons.** Gratitude is
  practised in the circle and enacted on the land, never assigned as
  content to be learned. This matches the anthroposophical position that
  moral capacities in childhood are cultivated through atmosphere and
  imitation, not instruction.
- **Festival as pedagogy.** The Evening of Light functions as a seasonal
  festival: the community crosses a threshold together and the crossing is
  marked with light. The form is ancient and local before it is Waldorf;
  the mountains kept it.
- **The garden/forest as curriculum.** The regeneration strand stands in
  the tradition of Waldorf *Gartenbau* — cultivation work as a formative
  subject in its own right — extended here from garden to forest.
- **Developmental calibration.** The 4A-Pathway (Section 3.3) rests on
  Steiner's developmental phases, used strictly as a developmental
  psychology: in the younger years the will leads — knowledge must arrive
  through the hands and senses before it can enter the mind; in
  adolescence feeling awakens — knowledge lands deepest when it matters,
  not merely when it is true; in late adolescence judgement matures and
  asks to act. The programme's age band spans all three, and facilitators
  calibrate the pathway's depth accordingly: with the youngest, genuine
  Awareness is the whole achievement of a day; with the oldest, Attitude
  presses toward Action.
- **Observation before explanation.** The insistence that the sensory
  encounter precede interpretation is at once a Waldorf commitment and a
  Goethean scientific one — *zarte Empirie*, letting the phenomenon speak
  before the observer imposes categories. For war-affected children this
  discipline has a second value: it protects a space in which nothing is
  demanded of them but attention.
- **Attention ripens into care.** The Goethean pedagogical literature
  records what the method produces without ever aiming at it: long,
  unhurried attention to a living thing becomes attachment, and attachment
  becomes the readiness to act on its behalf. The programme relies on
  exactly this unforced sequence — observation to connection to care — as
  the route by which ecological responsibility grows in a child without
  ever being assigned.
- **Embodiment as therapeutic ground.** Phenomenological psychology holds
  that the felt sense of self is anchored in the body's rhythms, its
  contact with the ground, and its attunement to others working alongside
  — and that reliable rhythm steadies a person's lived sense of time. The
  programme's insistence on hands in the soil, fixed daily form, and
  shoulder-to-shoulder work is therefore not tradition alone; it is
  aligned with the contemporary clinical understanding of how stability
  and connection are rebuilt.
- **The seven developmental needs of youth.** The programme answers, by
  design, the seven developmental needs of young adolescents identified by
  the Center for Early Adolescence: physical activity (the forest work
  itself); competence and achievement (a tree planted, a prototype built,
  recognition that names what each child carried); self-definition
  (patterns named in one's own words, one being kept through the season, a
  project chosen by the team); creative expression (Hack for Hope, story,
  festival); positive social interaction (the toloka form, the teams, the
  elders); structure and clear limits (the fixed daily rhythm and the
  standing rules); and meaningful participation (real work with real
  consequence — a forest that grows, a record that lasts, ideas a
  community takes up). No component exists for one need alone; each meets
  several at once, which is what sustains engagement.
- **Learning without levers.** The programme uses no reward and no
  punishment to produce participation. A child moved by reward or threat
  is obeying, not learning; the learning that lasts is carried by meaning,
  and the research on motivation bears this out — external rewards
  reliably corrode the inner wish to do the very thing they reward, while
  lasting engagement runs on autonomy, competence, and belonging, which is
  precisely what every component here provides. Engagement at Forest
  School is carried by real consequence instead: a tree that lives or dies
  by the tending, a record that outlasts the session, an idea a community
  takes up. The Seeds of Hope recognition is guarded by the same principle
  — it follows the work and describes what was true, and is never
  announced in advance as participation's price: a mirror, never a carrot.
  In this the programme stands in a worldwide family of learning places —
  forest kindergartens, open-air and democratic schools, craft and
  land-based education — that trust the child's own wish to learn and
  build the conditions in which that wish can work.

The learning architecture in Section 3 is drawn from the Erdpuls *Pattern
Language of Place* open educational resource collection (Pattern Discovery
Toolkit, EN v1.3), which documents the method, its ring structure, and its
developmental foundations in full and is published under the same CC BY-SA
4.0 license as this document. The method is designed and documented in
that collection, with its origin at an environmental education ground on
the German-Polish border in Brandenburg. Forest School carries the model
into a second landscape and a second language community — the same
architecture, with all cultural content and all safeguarding discipline
set by this place. The programme's inner side — its ethos, its tenets, and
the co-created eco-spiritual philosophy it serves — is stated in the
companion document "The Eco-Spiritual Philosophy of the Living Forest";
this document states the architecture, and the two stand together.

Two boundaries hold throughout:

1. **Method, not worldview.** The programme borrows pedagogical structure
   from this tradition. It teaches no doctrine, anthroposophical or
   otherwise, and all reflective practice remains non-confessional with
   silence and prayer optional.
2. **Cultural content is local.** Where the pedagogy is informed by
   Waldorf structure, the cultural substance — songs, stories, craft,
   agrarian knowledge, festival forms — is Hutsul and Ukrainian. The
   method serves the place; the place is not redecorated by the method.

## 6. Safeguarding

Child protection is a non-optional condition of the programme, not an
annex to it. In all public and application materials: no child is named or
depicted; no identifying detail is attached to any individual child; no
session dates, arrival or departure timing, routes, or headcounts are
published; programme description is given only in standing, undated
format. A wartime addendum hardens these rules further given the
programme's location. Qualified psychological and medical presence is
maintained on site throughout every session. All adults present during
sessions — including visiting elders and community members — take part
under the programme's safeguarding supervision and its mentoring
structure; participation in the intergenerational sessions is always in
the open, in the group, and within the standing rules. Informed choice
completes the protection: the programme's ethos and methods are stated
plainly in the materials parents read before giving written consent, so
that every family chooses with open eyes.

## 7. Signs the Work Is Working

The programme's outcomes are read the way the programme is run —
patiently, at the level of the standing practice, never through any
individual child:

- **The forest.** Area under active regeneration; seedlings planted and
  their survival across seasons; return of ground flora and species
  observed in the tended areas. The forest itself is the most honest
  evaluator the programme has.
- **The record.** Growth of the pattern-card record and the season-long
  measurement series across cohorts; patterns confirmed across seasons;
  patterns extended or contested by elders. A thickening record is a
  thickening relationship with the place.
- **The circle.** Elders returning; sessions requested again by the
  community; Hack for Hope ideas taken up beyond the camp.
- **The children.** Psychosocial progress is observed by the programme's
  qualified psychological staff using their standard professional
  instruments, held within the programme and never published; and the rate
  of return across seasons — children asking to come back to ground they
  have planted — is read as the programme's plainest sign.

- **In the community:** recurring elder participation; bonds between the

  youth-designed ideas taken up beyond the camp.
- **In the field:** a documented, transferable model joining psychosocial
  rehabilitation to ecological regeneration and youth empowerment,

## 9. Innovation Section — Condensed for the Application Form

In 2026, Forest School introduces an integrated innovation combining
emotional healing, ecological regeneration, and youth-led social action,
applying the approach of the community regeneration practice Soil and
Peace: peace built as infrastructure — capacities, relationships, and
living systems that remain — rather than as process. Its philosophy is
held in a Ukrainian proverb: *Наука в ліс не веде, а з лісу виводить* —
learning does not lead into the forest, it leads out of it. The programme
leads children into the literal forest so that learning can lead them out
of the figurative one — the forest of war, loss, and disorientation.

Daily reflection circles support emotional recovery through rhythm,
gratitude, and community connection. The learning method is the pattern
language of place: children discover and name the patterns of the forest,
the homestead, and the valley themselves, moving through a four-stage
pathway — Awareness, Acknowledgment, Attitude, Action — with the body as
first instrument and simple measurement as the second, and each cohort
inheriting and extending the pattern record of the last. A new hands-on
regeneration strand — the Forest of Peace — has children sowing, raising,
and planting native trees into ground they have cleared, composted, and
fed, so that ecological recovery is learned by doing it. The "Hack for
Hope" social innovation workshop empowers participants to develop, in
teams, creative responses to needs they have themselves observed, with
older and returning youth taking defined mentoring and co-facilitation
roles — youth empowerment built as a trajectory from participant to
co-leader. Intergenerational co-creation sessions bring elders of the
community and region into the shared work — knowledge, craft, and memory
flowing in every direction, and bonds forming between generations that
outlast the programme. The closing Evening of Light and Seeds of Hope
recognition send every child home seen, and carrying a living seed of the
work.

The innovation is the direction of the whole: care circulates. Children
who arrive as recipients of care become caregivers — to a forest, to a
community, to one another — and are cared for in return by the land
itself, which the programme treats not as wilderness apart from the human
but as a participant in the healing. Psychosocial rehabilitation is
thereby joined to agency, and healing to regeneration — of the child and
of the forest, as one process.
