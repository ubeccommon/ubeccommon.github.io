## 1. The question

Is the forest school, rightly seen, a hamlet — a small settlement of
households, work, learning, and land — run as a living lab, and
published as an open hamlet the way the modules are published as open
educational resources?

The forest school is not only a place for children. It is also a place
for adults — retreat and continuing education — and around it a small
settlement is planned: households living there, with work, learning,
and land held together. The open hamlet is that settlement, documented
as openly as the modules.

* * *

## 2. The frame

### 2.1 Living lab, as we use it

A descriptive frame, not an affiliation. The term is not protected, and
we claim no connection with any network or certification body. The
elements are drawn from the living lab literature. We use it to mean
five things together:

- work in a real-life setting;
- co-creation, with everyone who takes part — children and adults
  alike — a co-creator;
- many parties at the table — household, trades, elders, guests, other
  sites;
- many methods;
- and a cycle of trying, measuring, and revising.

The literature speaks of users. This project does not: everyone who
takes part is a co-creator, as the Erdpuls toolkit already holds for
pattern discovery. We keep the elements and depart from the word, as
the toolkit departs from Alexander. Section 11 still governs what is
published: children co-create in the living practice, and in the
record they appear only as the cohort, never as persons.

The maturity levels are the lab's cycle made explicit: L1 to L3 at this
site, L4 and L5 across sites.

### 2.2 Citizen science

Citizen science runs through it. Observation and measurement are done
by the people who live, work, and learn here, with the body first and
simple instruments second, as the Erdpuls toolkit sets out — and what
they record is open data, attribution only. The living lab is how the
place is run; citizen science is how it comes to know itself.

It already has three homes:

- **Modules** — the measure-first discipline: spring yield, soil, the
  battery through December. The measurement series in the pathway's
  toolchain are citizen science data.
- **Pattern cards** — the Instrumental Data field.
- **The hamlet** — residents and retreat guests keep the long series
  between sessions.

**The valley-resolution rule.** Citizen science data leaves the site
only at valley resolution, under the programme's instrument filter: no
coordinates, no exact dates tied to sessions, no account belonging to
or naming a child. Contributions to public platforms are made, if at
all, by an adult, with location obscured, after the wartime review.

This departs from the Erdpuls practice, which contributes to open
platforms freely. The departure is deliberate and named.

### 2.3 What a hamlet is in this landscape

As the literature holds it; every point below is for the elders to
confirm, correct, or withhold before it is written down as ours.

- **Dispersed settlement.** Separate homesteads scattered on the
  slopes, far from each other, placed near the pastures rather than
  gathered in the valley.
- **Village core and outliers.** A core in the valley — church, school,
  shop — with homesteads standing singly or grouped in outlying parts
  called kut, which belong to the village but lie far from it.
- **The enclosed homestead, ґражда (grazhda).** House and farm
  buildings joined by a high roofed wall into a closed yard with one
  gate; described as the form of the better-off, standing beside the
  open yard with freely placed buildings, and now seen mostly in
  museums.
- **Homestead, hayfield, forest, polonyna.** A land mostly of forest
  and meadow, with little arable ground; summer pasture on the
  polonyna, with its own seasonal buildings and enclosures, and the
  herd back down by the end of September.
- **толока (toloka).** Used in the programme for shared work; recorded
  in one study of the region also as family pasture held across
  generations near the homes — family land, not common land. Which
  sense this valley uses is for the elders.

The literature disagrees on the altitude of traditional dwellings. No
figure enters our text until it is resolved.

### 2.4 The word for it

| Word | What it carries | Use |
|---|---|---|
| присілок | small settlement belonging to a village | working term |
| кут | Hutsul outlying group of homesteads | for the elders |
| поселення | settlement; since 2024 also a legal term | avoid loosely |
| спільнота | the community of people | for the people |
| громада | also the territorial community | avoid |
| хутір | isolated farmstead | too small |

**Working title in Ukrainian:** відкритий присілок. The elders' word
replaces it if they give one.

**At another site.** Your own word replaces присілок; your own
knowledge-holders stand where "the elders" stand here; your own law
replaces every Ukrainian reference in this paper.

**A legal note, unchecked.** Law of Ukraine No. 3285-IX, in force from
26 January 2024, recognises town, selyshche, and village as the
categories of populated place, and is reported to give поселення a
narrow legal sense: a compact place of residence outside any populated
place, without a stable population. Read from secondary sources only;
the statute text is to be read before the word is used. A seasonal
retreat and education site may fall inside that sense, which makes the
word a legal question and not only a translation.

**One term avoided.** Родове помістя and the settlements built around
it belong to a particular movement, and a plain екопоселення carries
some of that association.

### 2.5 Three consequences for the component map

1. **Programme** gains an adult strand — retreat and continuing
   education — that the map does not yet have.
2. **Legal form and ownership** stays an L0 row and becomes a
   precondition: nobody lives on the site before it is settled.
3. **Safeguarding** must cover retreat guests and residents, not only
   visiting adults during sessions: a design question for the hamlet.

## 3. Two languages, kept apart

Alexander's A Pattern Language (1977) offers a language — 253
patterns, from region to construction detail — to be applied. The
Erdpuls toolkit departs from it: it arrives with a method for
discovering a place's own patterns, not a language to impose.

This paper asks both questions and keeps them apart:

- which of Alexander's patterns a forest school hamlet already carries
  or needs — Section 4;
- which patterns only this place, its household, and its elders can
  name — the discovery work, recorded on pattern cards, never
  pre-filled here.

Where an Alexander pattern and a likely local form pull against each
other, the tension is named and left for the place to answer.

### 3.1 Not one to one

No pattern language is taken over one to one — not Alexander's, and
not Erdpuls's. Alexander himself held all 253 patterns to be
hypotheses, open to change under new observation, and in The Oregon
Experiment (1975) asked for places to grow piecemeal rather than be
designed at once. His
list here is a set of prompts for looking, never a specification to
fill. A pattern of his that this place does not carry is not a lack;
it may be a sign that the place answers the same tension another way.

The place may also hold forms that are not patterns in his sense at
all. A pattern in his book is a context, a tension, and a resolution
in space. This landscape may speak in other forms: a rhythm — the
herd going up to the polonyna and back; a movement — the path from
homestead to spring; a relation held in custom rather than in
building. The record stays open to whatever form the place shows.

### 3.2 Thinking like a plant

The toolkit already takes its method from Goethe's study of the
metamorphosis of plants: one form, expressed differently wherever it
grows. The hamlet takes its way of growing from the same source.

- **Roots before shoots.** The baseline is walked and measured before
  anything is built — Phase 1 of the pathway.
- **Grow from where it stands.** From the existing buildings, the
  spring, the slope; build on the poorer ground and keep the best.
- **Grow toward light and water.** Siting follows sun and source,
  read on the ground, not on a plan.
- **Many small parts, no single centre.** Several small buildings and
  households rather than one block, so that the loss of one part does
  not end the whole.
- **Grow in season.** Building, like planting, keeps to its windows.
- **Sense everywhere.** Observation is distributed among everyone who
  lives, works, and learns here — citizen science as the plant's many
  small sensing points.
- **Shed and compost what fails.** A failed design is recorded, taken
  down, and returned to the record as what we got wrong.
- **Seed, not copy.** Another site does not copy this hamlet. It takes
  the method as a seed and grows its own form from its own ground —
  which is what L4 means.

### 3.3 Three spheres, as a lens

The hamlet's adults govern themselves with a lens taken from Rudolf
Steiner's social threefolding, used as method and not as worldview —
never taught to children, and asked of no family. Three questions
are put to every decision:

- **Cultural life — whose freedom is this?** Learning, the adult
  strand, the elders' culture, each family's faith, the pattern
  record: run by those who carry them, and funded by gift, never by
  sale. Where teaching is paid, as in retreats, the price is set in
  the economic sphere and the content stays with those who teach.
- **Rights life — what holds for everyone alike?** Standing rules,
  membership, consent, the tenure of the land, and safeguarding
  above all: equal for every person, and not for sale to money or
  pedagogy.
- **Economic life — what need, met by which association?** Meals,
  trades, shared work, the nursery, retreat income: agreed among
  those who produce and those who use.

The lens is not taken one to one. The theory is contested; the state
is part of the rights sphere, and under martial law more than ever;
and the place may hold its own forms of the same three, or others,
for the elders to name.

Where the same few people sit in all three spheres — as they will in a
hamlet of a few households — the lens still asks which question is
being decided, not who decides it.

**A caution from practice.** Camphill communities, partly organised
on these lines, lived for decades with co-workers who were unpaid and
housed as families. At one of them, from 2014, that model met charity
governance and employment rules, co-workers began to be made
employees, and residents' legal challenge was refused in 2015. No
economy here is designed to rest on unpaid residents without a
footing in Ukrainian labour law and the rules for children's
establishments.

**Where the spheres show in Alexander's prompts.**

- **Cultural:** 18 Network of Learning, 43 University as a
  Marketplace, 83 Master and Apprentices, 85 Shopfront Schools, 181
  The Fire, 185 Sitting Circle.
- **Rights:** 36 Degrees of Publicness, 44 Local Town Hall, 53 Main
  Gateways, 61 Small Public Squares, 67 Common Land, 69 Public Outdoor
  Room.
- **Economic:** 9 Scattered Work, 41 Work Community, 46 Market of Many
  Shops, 87 Individually Owned Shops, 91 Traveler's Inn, 148 Small
  Work Groups, 156 Settled Work, 157 Home Workshop, 177 Vegetable
  Garden, 178 Compost.
- **Where they meet, and where one most easily swallows another:** 45
  Necklace of Community Projects, 80 Self-Governing Workshops and
  Offices, 129 Common Areas at the Heart, 147 Communal Eating.

Patterns named here but not in Section 4 have not been walked; they
are for the reader to look up.

## 4. Alexander's patterns — prompts for the settlement walk

### 4.1 How to read this list

It is a set of prompts for the Phase 1 walk of the settlement, used
beside the pathway's baseline. Patterns are cited by number and name;
the descriptions are ours.

**Walk status** — distinct from a module's maturity level and from a
pattern card's status — is one of four:

- **present** — the loaded documents record it on the site;
- **absent** — the documents record that it is missing;
- **unknown** — nobody has looked yet;
- **planned** — a module at L1 already designs it.

A commitment in the programme proposal is not a record of the site. It
is noted beside an unknown, never counted as present.

Most rows are unknown. That is the finding: the baseline has not been
walked.

Absent is a description, never a verdict. Under Section 3.1, a
pattern this place does not carry is a question — what answers that
tension here? — not a gap to fill.

**Sacred sites, holy ground, and grave sites** — 24, 66, 70 — are
recorded nowhere, in workshop or publication, unless the elders ask.

### 4.6 Where the tension bites

1. **Cluster against dispersal.** 37 House Cluster and 35 Household
   Mix assume a face-to-face group of dwellings. The literature holds
   the Hutsul form as dispersed homesteads, grouped at most as a kut.
   The place may hold a different answer — dispersed households joined
   by paths and shared work.
2. **Gradient against enclosure.** 127 Intimacy Gradient and 36
   Degrees of Publicness assume a soft gradient; the grazhda is a hard
   wall with one gate. The literature also reports, in a few
   homesteads, a kept front yard and a working back yard inside it — a
   gradient within the enclosure, and a candidate pattern for the
   elders to name or not.
3. **Valley against slope.** 4 Agricultural Valleys protects valley
   farmland. Here the counterpart may be the hayfield. For discovery.
4. **Common land and toloka.** The one study cited records toloka as
   family-held pasture, not common land — it may pull against 67
   Common Land rather than match it. Whether anything here is held in
   common is for the elders.
5. **Sacred sites and graves.** Patterns Alexander would have us
   protect by rule and by plan. Here they enter no plan unless the
   elders ask.

## 5. The bridge

### 5.1 What is true

Modules are not simply the construction-scale implementation of
patterns, and patterns are not a layer above the component map. The
relation is many to many: one module touches several patterns, and one
pattern draws on several modules. Patterns cut across the map as a
second way of reading the same place — the map says what is built, the
patterns say why and where it belongs.

Evidence flows back. A module measured through a season is citizen
science data for the pattern cards it touches. Patterns give modules
their reason; modules give patterns their evidence.

### 5.2 Three kinds of pattern

| Kind | Named by | Cited as |
|---|---|---|
| The place's | household, co-creators, elders | card name |
| Alexander's | the book, as a prompt | number and name |
| A module's design rules | us, as designers | the module's text |

A module's own design rules — the water module's four rules — stay
inside the module and are tested by its maturity level, not by
pattern status.

### 5.3 The pattern line

Every module carries, under its Connections line:

- **Grows from** and **Feeds** — the place's patterns, by card name,
  once the card exists; until then, "not yet named";
- **Resonates with** — Alexander's, by number and name, as prompts;
- **In tension with** — any pattern the module pulls against.

Only the place's patterns are grown from and fed. Alexander's are
resonance, never specification. The project's adapted pattern card
carries the return line, **Implemented by**, naming the modules. The
Erdpuls card is not edited.

### 5.4 The water module as the example

- **Grows from:** not yet named. Two questions for the elders before
  the spring box is built — how homesteads here stand to their spring,
  and whether this spring is held in any way that decides how it may
  be touched. The SPADOK heritage page on the Hutsul dwelling places
  the outbuildings of a yard by relief, road, garden, and distance to
  the spring: a candidate place pattern, the elders' to name.
- **Resonates with:** 207 Good Materials, 83 Master and Apprentices,
  104 Site Repair.
- **In tension with:** 64 Pools and Streams — the buried cistern and
  piped main take water out of sight; the spring and catchment are
  where it can stay visible. 25 Access to Water — the catchment fence
  keeps people back from water the pattern would open to them.

### 5.5 Status and level

Pattern status — first observation, confirmed, contested, refined,
archived — says what is known about the place. Maturity level says
what is built. They meet in one place only: a module at L3 is a data
source for its patterns' cards. No card is confirmed on a module's
evidence alone; it still needs independent observations across groups
and seasons.

## 6. The open hamlet

*Decided 2026-09-27.*

### 6.1 What the open hamlet is

Two things published side by side, neither complete without the
other:

- **The pattern language of this place** — discovered, not imposed;
  named by the household, the co-creators, and the elders; recorded on
  cards; told once a year as a narrative, as the Assembly Guide sets
  out.
- **The modules** — how the place is built and run, each carrying its
  pattern line.

The hamlet itself is never published as a plan. What travels is the
language and the modules: a seed, not a copy.

### 6.2 What is published, and what is not

- **Published, when each is ready:** modules from L1, marked; the
  pattern language as a narrative, at valley resolution, after the
  year's synthesis; schematic diagrams of relationship between parts
  of the hamlet.
- **Not published:** any site plan with distances, until the
  decision on naming the site. The site is the hamlet: dispersed
  homesteads on a slope, drawn with distances, are the most
  identifying artefact there is; relationships alone are published.
  Construction drawings of a single structure keep their dimensions.
- **Not published:** pattern cards in full — they carry the most
  detail of place. They stay in the workshop; the narrative draws on
  them.
- **Not published:** anything of the elders' without their consent,
  on their terms; anything of a resident household's life without
  that household's own Gate 1.

### 6.3 The pattern card, adapted

The Erdpuls card, with six rules for this project:

- no child's name; discoveries attributed to the cohort;
- place recorded at ring level only — body, homestead, garden and
  forest edge, village, valley — never by coordinates;
- names in the discoverers' own language first;
- an **Implemented by** line naming the modules;
- an open **Form** field — pattern, rhythm, movement, relation, or
  another the place shows — so the card does not force Alexander's
  shape on what is found;
- an optional **Sphere** field — cultural, rights, economic, or where
  they meet — left empty when none fits.

The Erdpuls card lives in the Pattern Discovery Toolkit, EN v1.3, in the
Erdpuls OER collection. The adapted card is
standards/template_pattern_card_EN_v0_1.md.

### 6.4 The component map

Four changes:

- **A pattern layer across all groups** — not a group, because
  patterns cut across the map. It lives in the pattern record and in
  each module's pattern line.
- **A seventh group, settlement** — for what only a settlement needs:
  its form and siting, resident dwellings, paths and gateway, common
  land, and how the settlement governs itself and who belongs to it.
  All at L0.
- **Self-governance, designed threefold** (decided 2026-09-27): those
  who teach decide pedagogy; all members decide the standing rules,
  each with one voice; the associations of those who produce and use
  decide the economy. Safeguarding sits in the rights sphere, beyond
  the reach of the other two.
- **One row added to programme:** the adult strand, retreat and
  continuing education, at L0.

The map grows from thirty-four modules to forty: two at L2, sixteen at
L1, twenty-two at L0. The new rows stay visibly empty.

### 6.5 The pathway

- **Section 1** names the settlement as part of what is being built.
- **Section 3** gains the pattern line and the three kinds of pattern.
- **Section 5** gains the settlement group and the adult strand row.
- **Section 10** gains a step for the replicating site: walk its own
  settlement with the prompts and begin its own pattern record.
- **Section 11** gains the plan rule of 6.2, the card rule, and a
  summary of the safeguarding tiers a replicating site can read.
- **Section 14** gains, in its second step, the settlement walk with
  Alexander's prompts and the start of the pattern record.
- **Section 15** gains six open decisions: legal form before
  residents, with land in trust as one option; each resident
  household's Gate 1; safeguarding for retreat guests and residents;
  how much of the adult strand is published; whether released modules
  carry the author's name; and the capacity and calendar figures. The
  last two were decided the same day: both may be published.

### 6.6 The template and the repository

- **Template:** the pattern line under Connections, and the seed
  sentence under Adaptation notes — applied 2026-09-27.
- **Repository:** a settlement group folder beside the six; a patterns/
  folder in the workshop for cards and the yearly narrative, with the
  cards never released; pattern files named
  pattern_[name]_[LANG]_v[major]_[minor].md.

  Alexander's patterns are prompts, and the place may show forms that
  are not patterns in his sense.
- **Thinking like a plant:** the hamlet grows piecemeal, from what is
  there, in season, as a seed and not a copy.
- **The bridge:** three kinds of pattern; the pattern line in every
  module — grows from, feeds, resonates with, in tension with — added
  to the template and the water module.
- **Three spheres:** social threefolding as a lens for the adults'
  self-governance, never a doctrine; an optional Sphere field on the
  adapted card; the self-governance module designed threefold.
- **The open hamlet:** published as the place's pattern language and
  the modules, never as a plan; hamlet-scale plans as relationships
  only, without distances, until the site-naming decision; pattern
  cards kept in the workshop, only the yearly narrative released.
- **The map:** a seventh group, settlement; a pattern layer across all
  groups; the adult strand added to programme. Forty modules.
- **Legal form:** land held in trust, out of the market, named as one
  option to weigh; not decided.
- **Names and figures:** released modules carry the author's name and
  the initiative's name; capacity, session length, and use days may be
  published, as standing format and never beside a date.

## 9. Stress tests, 2026-09-27

Three independent reviews, each by a reader who had not seen the work
being produced.

**Safeguarding and aggregation.** A hostile reader with the workshop in
hand. Found: the author's name leads to other, dated writing about the
place; a tool in the workshop carried material from that writing; the
capacity figure sits in a releasable module while its publication is
undecided; use days and session length together give the programme's
calendar; the old plan rule, relationships and distances, stood unamended
in four files; the release list refused too little. All corrected or
raised as open decisions; the name and the figures were then decided for
publication, the tool split, and a release script written.

**Replication.** A small mountain initiative in another country trying
to use the method. Found: the safeguarding tiers it rests on sit in a
document it will never see; counts and references that disagreed
between files; folders described as existing that do not; one word,
status, doing three jobs; a paid retreat with no home among the three
spheres; no step for its own settlement walk. All corrected.

**Evidence.** Every claim checked against sources. Found: six of the
Alexander descriptions misstated their pattern — 7, 25, 47, 71, 91,
197 — and the water module's tension was named with the wrong
pattern; toloka as family pasture pulls against common land rather
than matching it; statuses of present and planned rested on a funding
proposal or on L0 rows. All corrected. Checked and found right: every
pattern number and name, the forty-module count, the Botton Village
dates, the law's dates, the altitude disagreement.
