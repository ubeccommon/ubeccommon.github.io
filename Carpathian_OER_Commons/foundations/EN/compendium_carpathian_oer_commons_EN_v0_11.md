## Part 0 — The commons at a glance

### 1. What the commons is

The Carpathian OER Commons is an open guide to how one place in the Hutsul
highlands of the Ukrainian Carpathians is built and run: a residential
forest school for children and youth, a place of retreat and continuing
education for adults, and a small settlement growing around them — a
hamlet, присілок, the working term until the elders give theirs
[for the elders].

The guide is written as modules, forty-three of them, each with the same
shape and each carrying a maturity level that states what may be claimed
from it. Beside the modules it carries the place's own pattern language,
discovered and not imposed. Today two modules stand at a level the site's
own experience backs, fifteen are at design stage, five — the market
garden, the compost, micro-hydro, wood heat, and the Carpathian
Earthship — are published at L0 as marked drafts, and twenty-one are
empty rows, left visibly empty.

**The initiative.** The commons grows out of Soil and Peace, a co-created
community regeneration practice based on one conviction: that the hand
that tends soil and the hand that tends what lies between people are the
same hand. The project uses, and helps to develop, the framework of the
Ubuntu Bioregional Economic Commons (UBEC) DAO, and draws on the Erdpuls
open educational resources as worked examples. Section 6 says more.

**Open educational resources.** Documents are released under CC BY-SA 4.0,
drawings under a hardware licence still to be chosen, and measurement data
openly, with attribution only. That places the guide inside the definition
UNESCO adopted in 2019: "learning, teaching and research materials in any
format and medium that reside in the public domain or are under copyright
that have been released under an open license, that permit no-cost access,
re-use, re-purpose, adaptation and redistribution by others." The licence
makes the guide open. It does not by itself make it a commons.

**Why a commons.** In Elinor Ostrom's work a commons is a shared resource
held by the people who depend on it, under rules they make and can change
(Governing the Commons, 1990). The resources she studied — pastures,
fisheries, irrigation — are used up by use: what one takes, another
cannot. A guide is not; reading it takes nothing from it. What can be used
up here is different: the trust of the household the guide describes, the
consent of the elders whose knowledge it draws on, the safety of a place
in a country at war, and the honesty of the record. Those are what this
commons has to govern. Knowledge treated as a commons is the subject of
later work by Charlotte Hess and Elinor Ostrom (Understanding Knowledge as
a Commons, 2007), and that is the reference when governance is built — in
Phase 5 of the pathway (Section 17), and not before. A commons with one
member needs no constitution.

**The measure.** Borrowed from Open Source Ecology and narrowed (Section
9.1): **the guide is complete when another site has built from it, in
another valley, and their figures sit in it beside ours.** Not when every
module is written, not when it is translated into six languages, not when
it is published. A guide with six modules and one honest replication is
further along than thirty modules read by nobody. It is the only measure
this document uses for the commons.

What changes in the people who take part is not measured by the guide and
is not published. The programme's qualified staff hold that, inside the
programme. The commons is measured by whether what it describes can be
built again elsewhere.

### 2. Safeguarding governs everything here

The guide is a published artefact, so the project's child-protection rules
apply to every line of it, and the aggregation test applies across
documents: a drawing in one, a photograph in another, and a date elsewhere
can together place a site that no single document placed. When in doubt,
the hold closes.

- **No child in any module.** Not in a photograph, not in a caption, not
  in an example, not in a headcount, not as texture. Programme modules
  describe standing format only. Children co-create in the living
  practice; in the record they appear only as the cohort, never as
  persons.
- **Publish the method, not the place.** Site plans in a published
  document are schematic — relationships only, without distances, names,
  or the terrain that identifies a valley, until the decision on naming
  the site. The site is the hamlet: dispersed homesteads on a slope, drawn
  with distances, are the most identifying artefact there is, and would
  place a valley. Construction drawings of a single structure keep their
  dimensions.
- **Photographs** show structures and systems: no faces, no signage, no
  distinctive ridgelines, no metadata.
- **Data** leaves the site only at valley resolution: no coordinates, no
  dates tied to a session, no account belonging to or naming a child
  (Section 9.5).
- **What keeps people safe** — the shelter, the safety link, the
  battery, the generator, and the fuel store — is described in standing
  format: what each must do and how it is sized, never where it stands,
  what fuel is held, or when anything is run. The shelter's own supply
  is published as a principle and a method, without its figures.
- **Regulatory and application material** that names an entity travels to
  funders, not to the repository.
- **Pattern cards** stay in the workshop. Only the yearly narrative of the
  place's pattern language is published, at valley resolution.
- **The elders and the households.** Nothing of the elders' is published
  without their consent, on their terms. Nothing of a resident household's
  life is published without that household's own Gate 1.
- **Gate 1 throughout.** The household decides what of its own life is
  described, module by module.
- **Wartime review before every release**, not once at the start. What was
  safe to publish in one season may not be in the next.
- **A line held beyond the rules.** Children, wartime, a named doorstep, a
  fixed week, and family circumstance, in one piece, are never published,
  whatever the rules at the time allow.

The guide is publishable because infrastructure is publishable. The
programme modules are the difficult ones, and the right instinct there is
to publish the architecture of the safeguarding and very little of the
programme's daily life.

**For a replicating site,** which will not see the project's working
rules: Tier 1 — no child named, dated, counted, or placed, ever; Tier 2 —
anything more only through consent gates; Gate 1 — each household decides
what of its own life is described.

### 3. How the work is organised

**Two repositories, one direction.** The workshop is a private repository
on GitHub, under ubeccommon. Everything is written, checked, and argued
over there — drafts, sources, L0 and L1 work, open decisions. The
publication is a collection, Carpathian_OER_Commons, in the public
repository ubeccommon.github.io, beside the Erdpuls collection Pattern
Language of Place, and served by the same machinery: folder pages with a
language switcher, a viewer for the markdown, deployment on every push.
Material moves one way only, workshop to publication, and nothing is
written directly in the publication. Every clone is a full local copy, for
when the connection fails, and the hosting costs the household nothing.

**Why the release step is a gate.** The publication repository is public,
and git keeps everything ever committed to it: a file copied there by
mistake is public for good, even if deleted the next minute. So nothing is
released by hand. A release is a dated list of files; a script copies only
what is on the list, refuses any module below L1 that is not a
marked draft, any Gate 1 or wartime
review line left unfilled, and a foundations document without both, and
checks every file against a list of place names kept outside both
repositories. The printable PDFs are built after the copy and checked
against the same list.

**The layout.** The same in both repositories, minus what never leaves the
workshop.

- **foundations/** — this document and, in grounding/, the three grounding
  documents (Section 4), by language: foundations/EN/, with UK/ and DE/
  beside it.
- **modules/** — the guide itself, language first, then group:
  modules/EN/utilities/, with UK/ and DE/ beside it; on the public site, a
  pdf/ folder in each language.
- **standards/** — the module template and the pattern card template,
  published; the repository conventions, the site parameters, the checker,
  and the release script, in the workshop only.
- **drawings/** and **data/** — planned: source drawings with their
  exports, and measurement series, by group. They carry no language.
- **patterns/** — planned, in the workshop: the place's pattern cards,
  which stay there for good, and the yearly narrative drawn from them,
  which is released.
- **releases/** and **sources/** — in the workshop only: the release
  lists, and the three working-paper guides the modules are converted
  from.

**Toolchain.** The guide is written in the same markdown standard as
everything else in this project, the Erdpuls standard, and checked by the
guide's own checker, standards/check_oer_standard.py, before every commit.
Other writing about the place stays where it is; the guide is a separate
thing with a separate rhythm, because testimony and technical
documentation age differently.

- **Text:** markdown, one file per module, checker before commit; file
  names carry the language and the version, as
  module_utilities_water_EN_v0_2.md.
- **Drawings:** SVG or FreeCAD, source files committed beside the exports.
  A PDF of a drawing is not a drawing.
- **Measurements:** plain CSV, one series per file, with a header stating
  instrument, place at valley resolution, and who took it.
- **Photographs:** metadata stripped, filenames neutral, reviewed against
  Section 2 before they go anywhere near a server.
- **Figures shared between modules** — capacity, litres per person, the
  exchange rate — are declared once, in a site parameters file, and cited
  by name. A change there is carried through every module that cites it in
  the same commit. Whether each value may be published is decided value by
  value.
- **Releases:** a dated snapshot when a group of modules reaches L2, so
  that a replicating site can cite a fixed version.

**Languages.** English is canonical and settles first; other languages are
siblings with a stated revision date, never blocking publication.
Ukrainian follows close behind and is corrected over time rather than held
back. German is published beside them. The Ukrainian and German texts now
published are machine translations, marked as such and provisional until a
native reader has reviewed them; where they differ, the English holds.
Polish, Romanian, Slovak, and Hungarian come only when a partner in that
country takes on the translation as their own work — a machine-translated
module with no native reader is worse than none, because it looks checked.
The Ukrainian and German machine translations, decided 2026-09-28, are the
stated exception: marked provisional on their face, with the English
holding, until a native reader has reviewed them.

**Versions.** A document carries its version in its name and in its front
matter. A change of content steps the version; the older file leaves the
public site, and the workshop keeps its history.

**Where the work stands, 2026-10-06.** Phase 0 is largely done: the
module template, the pattern card template, the repository conventions,
the site parameters, the checker, and the release script. Of the three
working-paper guides, water is fully converted, into its water module
and its rainwater and nursery irrigation module, both at L1, and
published in English, Ukrainian, and German; sanitation is converted
but for its washroom row — its terra preta and biochar rows are modules
at L1, published in the same three languages, with their PDFs, and so
are its toilets row and its greywater, reed bed, and pond row, and its
wood heat row is carried in the wood heat module; energy is fully
converted, into its energy module at L1 and its micro-hydro and wood
heat modules at L0 as marked drafts, published in the same three
languages, with their PDFs. The
market garden, the first module of the open hamlet, is published at L0
as a marked draft, v0.5, in English,
Ukrainian, and German, with its PDFs; the compost module, which feeds
it, is published beside it at L0 as a marked draft, v0.2, in the same
three languages, with its PDFs. Phase 1 has not begun: the
baseline has not been walked, and the pattern record has not been
started. The first buildings module, the Carpathian Earthship, is
published at L0 as a marked draft, v0.1, in English, Ukrainian, and
German, with its PDFs and four sheets of drawings.

## Part I — Philosophy: why the commons exists and what it stands on

### 6. Background

**The initiative.** The place the guide describes is where the conviction
of Soil and Peace (Section 1) is being worked out, on the ground, with a
household and its neighbours.

**Peace as infrastructure.** Soil and Peace takes the phrase from
peacebuilding, where an infrastructure for peace means the relationships
and structures that carry a peace beyond its agreements, and reads it at
the scale of a household and its ground. John Paul Lederach proposed the
idea in Building Peace (1997), out of mediation work in Nicaragua. In his
words, "we are not merely interested in 'ending' something that is not
desired. We are oriented toward the building of relationships that in
their totality form new patterns, processes, and structures." Our reading
changes the scale and the material — a spring, a forest, a record, a
working relationship between neighbours — and keeps his point: the aim is
not only to end what is unwanted but to build what holds.

**The guide is the checkable case.** That peace grows from tended ground
is a conviction, and this document does not claim it as a finding. One
part of it can be checked. Documentation that others build from, and that
outlasts the people who wrote it, is infrastructure in the plainest sense.
If a second site builds from the guide and its figures come back into it,
that much will have been shown — for the guide, not for peace.

**Why a guide.** The place is being built in wartime, in mountains that
run through seven states: Ukraine, Romania, Poland, Slovakia, Hungary,
Serbia, and Czechia. Other initiatives in the range face the same
questions — water, power, sanitation, winter, safeguarding, how to hold
land — with the same scarcity of money and time. Many published projects
describe what worked and leave out what failed and what it cost. The guide
exists so that the next site does not repeat the expensive mistakes, and
so that what was found here is offered as method, never as a template.

**What the war changes.** The war is the ground the work stands on, not
its subject. It sets hard limits on what can be published (Section 2): no
child in any form, no site named while the decision on naming it is open,
no plan that places a valley, no data finer than valley resolution, and a
wartime review before every release. Those limits are not an inconvenience
to be engineered around. They are part of the method, and a site in
peacetime may keep them for its own reasons.

### 7. What the commons stands on

Every idea in this document grew somewhere else first. What we stand on
was laid down by others, the way soil is — in layers, from what lived
before: a practice of tending ground, a way of documenting that others can
build from, a way of looking that lets a place speak first, words lent by
peoples who were here long before this work. We receive them as the ground
receives what falls on it — slowly, changed by what they meet, until they
feed something that was not there before. We take nothing whole. We take
as a root takes: only what it can turn into its own growth, and only where
it stands. What is ours is not the ideas but the ground they have fallen
on — and the honesty to name where each one grew.

The documents this one draws on speak in two registers. The grounding
documents — the philosophy of the living forest, the comparative survey,
the programme proposal (Section 4) — speak from conviction. The guide
speaks from measurement. The commons needs both, and neither may borrow
the other's authority.

**Held as ground.** Four convictions, stated as convictions:

- **Care circulates.** We hold that where work is done together, care runs
  in every direction until no one is only a giver or only a receiver. The
  guide's forms of it are concrete: joint attribution, the household's
  time counted as contribution, cultural work to be funded by gift. The
  guide does not say that care is the only currency. Every module carries
  a cost line in hryvnia, and it would be untrue.
- **Land and people are regenerated together.** The work on the ground and
  the work between people are one work, seen from two sides. The guide
  measures the ground side — a spring's yield, seedling survival,
  returning species — and says nothing about the other.
- **Peace is grown as infrastructure.** Section 6.
- **A place teaches, when it is attended to.** Every place has its own
  pattern language; the task is to notice it, not to invent it (Section
  9.2).

**Not claimed.** The guide does not claim that the forest heals, that the
method changes the people who take part, or that the place has become what
it hopes to be. Those are the reasons the work is done. They are not
results the work has shown, and no module is written as if they were.

**The bridge.** One practice already carries both registers at once: the
dialogue between the body and the instrument. A hand in the soil says
sheltered here, exposed there; a thermometer gives a number; neither
replaces the other, and the gap between them is where the learning is. The
commons takes that as its discipline for everything it writes: a
conviction may say what to look at; only a measurement may say what was
found.

### 8. Principles

#### 8.1 Not one to one

No language, method, or tradition is taken over whole — not Alexander's,
not Steiner's, not the Erdpuls method's, not any people's. And none is
offered whole: a site that builds from the guide takes modules, not the
model, and grows its own form from its own ground. The rule runs both
ways, and that is its point.

Its source is Alexander's caveat on his own book: "each pattern may be
looked upon as a hypothesis, like one of the hypotheses of science" (A
Pattern Language, 1977, p. xv). What we change is its direction. He
applied it to what he offered; we apply it also to what we take.

#### 8.2 With, not for

MIT, nicht FÜR — the ethical spine of Soil and Peace, kept in its German:
building with a place and its people rather than for them. In the guide it
governs three relations. Between the author and the place: the guide
describes our own work, never a place's life on its behalf. Between the
guide and its readers: replication is an invitation, never an export.
Between the work and those who take part: everyone is a co-creator
(Section 8.4). Work that begins as done with people and lasts long enough
becomes work they lead; that is what "with" means over time, not a third
term.

#### 8.3 Honest before useful

A module that says "we have not tested this" is worth more than one that
reads well. Every module states its level at the top, from L0, an idea, to
L5, improved by others and merged back, and readers act on the level, not
on the prose (Section 15). Every figure is measured, quoted, or marked
[set] or [assumed]; a cost without a date is a rumour. The failure of open
documentation is work at one level published in the language of a higher
one. This document is held to the same rule, and Section 12 applies it to
its own convictions.

#### 8.4 Co-creators

Everyone who takes part — children and adults alike, the household, the
trades, the elders, guests, residents, the people of other sites — is a
co-creator. The living-lab literature says users. The Erdpuls method does
not, and the commons follows it, because a pattern language that people
have not discovered and named themselves is someone else's, imposed.
Attribution follows the work: where it was joint, the credit is joint. An
open licence that quietly credits shared work to one author is a theft
dressed as generosity.

#### 8.5 Not ours to give

Knowledge that belongs to a people or to particular persons is not open
because someone wrote it down. Hutsul practice — forms, songs, craft, the
knowledge of a valley — enters the guide only with the consent of those
whose knowledge it is, on terms they set, and some of it will not enter at
all [for the elders]. The default is to describe our own use of a practice
and point to its holders, rather than publish the practice as if it were
ours to give. Sacred sites, holy ground, and graves are recorded nowhere,
in the workshop or in publication, unless the elders ask. The same holds
for every tradition named in Section 10: we say whose it is and how we use
it, and we do not speak for it.

#### 8.6 Across the borders

The commons is named for the range, not for a country. The frame is
bioregional. Peter Berg and Raymond Dasmann introduced the bioregion and
reinhabitation as paired terms in "Reinhabiting California" (The
Ecologist, 1977), and wrote, in the essay as reprinted the following year,
that "the final boundaries of a bioregion are best described by the people
who have lived within it, through human recognition of the realities of
living-in-place." Here that sentence is a rule: the boundaries of the
place, like its names, are for the people who live in it and for the
elders to draw [for the elders].

A cross-border frame for the range already exists in the Carpathian
Convention. It is worth understanding before inventing a network: it has
working groups, funded programmes, and a habit of cooperation that
predates this project. Its current standing is to be checked before it is
cited in any application.

What travels across the borders: sizing methods, the order of decisions,
failure modes and the record of what failed, the maturity discipline, the
safeguarding architecture. What does not travel, and must be localised by
whoever is there: regulation — sanitary, building, water, child
protection, all national; prices and trades; species and planting
calendars; the language of the programme; the relationship with a local
authority, which is always personal.

Each module therefore carries one regulatory row per country, dated and
signed by whoever checked it. An empty row stays visibly empty, so that a
reader in Romania can see at a glance that nobody has checked Romania.
And each module says at its head that permits, registrations, or
approvals may be required wherever it is built, and that a reader
elsewhere checks their own rules before building.

### 9. Where the method comes from

#### 9.1 Documentation as the deliverable

Open Source Ecology, founded by Marcin Jakubowski in 2003, sets out to
publish open designs for the fifty industrial machines it counts as needed
for a small civilisation with modern comforts, the Global Village
Construction Set — built and tested on its own farm, designed to be
modular, repairable, and made locally at a fraction of commercial cost.
Four of its habits are taken.

- **Documentation is the deliverable.** A thing built and not documented
  has not been finished. OSE treats a demonstrated, replicable production
  model as the completion metric, not a working prototype.
- **Modularity.** Independent parts that interoperate, so that a reader
  takes the two modules they need without swallowing the whole.
- **Lifetime design and serviceability.** Built to be repaired by the
  people who use it, with the documentation that makes repair possible.
- **Replication is the measure of success.** Not how good the original is,
  but how quickly others can build and improve on it.

Four are not taken.

- **The machines.** This is not a fabrication project. The technologies
  here are a cistern, a reed bed, a wood stove, a battery, a nursery —
  mostly older than industry and mostly built by local trades.
- **The completeness claim.** No set of fifty anything. The guide covers
  what this site actually does, and says plainly where it stops.
- **The rhetoric.** No post-scarcity, no new economy, no
  civilisation-building language. The register here is a household in a
  war, describing what it built and what it cost.
- **Open by default, everywhere.** A hardware project can publish
  everything. This one cannot: children, a wartime location, and knowledge
  that is not ours to license all set limits (Section 2).

#### 9.2 Discovery, not imposition

Christopher Alexander and his colleagues set out 253 patterns in A Pattern
Language (1977), from the region to the construction detail. In their
definition, a pattern "describes a problem which occurs over and over
again in our environment, and then describes the core of the solution to
that problem." The book offers a language to be applied.

The Erdpuls method departs from him once: it arrives with a way of
discovering a place's own pattern language, not with a language to impose.
The commons departs a second time. Alexander's patterns are prompts for
looking, never a specification (Section 26). A pattern of his that the
place does not carry is not a lack; it is a question — what answers that
tension here — and may be a sign that the place answers the same tension
another way.

The place may also hold forms that are not patterns in his sense at all. A
pattern in his book is a context, a tension, and a resolution in space.
This landscape may speak in other forms: a rhythm — the herd going up to
the polonyna and back; a movement — the path from homestead to spring; a
relation held in custom rather than in building. The record stays open to
whatever form the place shows.

Three kinds of pattern are kept apart: the place's, named by the
household, the co-creators, and the elders; Alexander's, cited by number
and name as prompts; and a module's own design rules, which stay inside
the module and are tested by its maturity level (Section 27.2).

#### 9.3 Delicate empiricism

"Es gibt eine zarte Empirie, die sich mit dem Gegenstand innigst identisch
macht und dadurch zur eigentlichen Theorie wird" — there is a delicate
empiricism that makes itself wholly one with its object, and so becomes
theory in the true sense (Goethe, Maximen und Reflexionen; the translation
is ours). A second maxim gives the order of work: no phenomenon explains
itself; only many, surveyed together and methodically ordered, give
something that may count as theory.

The Erdpuls method takes from Goethe the order of knowing: observation
before explanation, the body as the first instrument, simple measurement
as the second. Later writers, Henri Bortoft among them, have set Goethe's
way of seeing out in stages; those stages are theirs, not Goethe's, and
are cited as such where they are used.

#### 9.4 Growing like a plant

Goethe's Metamorphosis of Plants (1790) — one form, expressed differently
wherever it grows — gives the Erdpuls method its way of looking, and the
settlement its way of growing.

- **Roots before shoots.** The place is walked and measured before
  anything is built — Phase 1 of the pathway.
- **Grow from where it stands.** From the existing buildings, the spring,
  the slope; build on the poorer ground and keep the best.
- **Grow toward light and water.** Siting follows sun and source, read on
  the ground, not on a plan.
- **Many small parts, no single centre.** Several small buildings and
  households rather than one block, so that the loss of one part does not
  end the whole.
- **Grow in season.** Building, like planting, keeps to its windows.
- **Sense everywhere.** Observation is distributed among everyone who
  lives, works, and learns here — citizen science as the plant's many
  small sensing points.
- **Shed and compost what fails.** A failed design is recorded, taken
  down, and returned to the record as what we got wrong.
- **Seed, not copy.** Another site does not copy this place. It takes the
  method as a seed and grows its own form from its own ground — which is
  what L4 means.

Alexander asked the same of a community in The Oregon Experiment (1975):
piecemeal growth rather than a plan built at once.

This is a metaphor for method, not a claim about plants. The phrase echoes
Aldo Leopold's "thinking like a mountain" (A Sand County Almanac, 1949).
The picture of a living body with no single centre, sensing everywhere, is
close to Stefano Mancuso's argument about plant intelligence, which other
biologists dispute. Neither is used as evidence.

#### 9.5 Citizen science and the living lab

The term citizen science has two origins, reached independently. Alan
Irwin (Citizen Science, 1995) meant a science answerable to citizens'
concerns and knowledge that citizens produce themselves; Rick Bonney
(1996) meant members of the public contributing data to a research
project. Here, observation and measurement are done by the people who
live, work, and learn at the place, with the body first and simple
instruments second, and the practice stands nearer Irwin's sense. What
they record is open data, attribution only. The living lab is how the
place is run; citizen science is how it comes to know itself.

It has three homes:

- **Modules** — the measure-first discipline: spring yield, soil, the
  battery through December. The measurement series in the toolchain are
  citizen science data.
- **Pattern cards** — the Instrumental Data field.
- **The hamlet** — residents and retreat guests keep the long series
  between sessions.

**The valley-resolution rule.** It departs from the openness the other
sense assumes, and from the Erdpuls method, whose documents provide for
contributing freely to open platforms. Data leaves the site only at valley
resolution, under the programme's instrument filter: no coordinates, no
dates tied to a session, no account belonging to or naming a child. Any
contribution to a public platform is made, if at all, by an adult, with
the location obscured, after the wartime review. The departure is
deliberate, and a replicating site can weigh its two reasons for itself: a
country at war, and children who cannot consent to being placed.

**Living lab** is used as a descriptive frame, not an affiliation. The
term is not protected, and no connection is claimed with any outside
network or certification body. Its elements are drawn from the living-lab
literature, and it means five things together: work in a real setting;
co-creation; many parties at the table — household, trades, elders,
guests, other sites; many methods; and a cycle of trying, measuring, and
revising. The literature speaks of users; the commons keeps the elements
and departs from the word (Section 8.4). The maturity levels are the lab's
cycle made explicit: L1 to L3 at this site, L4 and L5 across sites.

#### 9.6 Head, heart, and hand

Learning with head, heart, and hand is Johann Heinrich Pestalozzi's
(1746–1827). Rudolf Steiner's thinking, feeling, and willing is a later
parallel; the Erdpuls method draws on it as a developmental psychology,
not as a worldview, and the commons needs only Pestalozzi's form.

The four stages the Erdpuls method uses — Awareness, Acknowledgment,
Attitude, Action — are its own. The method is designed and documented in
the Erdpuls OER collection, in the Pattern Discovery Toolkit, EN v1.3, and
the Pattern Language Assembly Guide, EN v1.2. It is claimed here as
designed and documented, and no more.

### 10. Borrowed words

Each word below belongs to someone. This section says whose, and how the
commons holds it.

**Toloka.** Neighbours doing together a piece of work none could do alone,
repaid with a feast. The practice is not only Carpathian: its names run
from talka in Latvian and Lithuanian and talgud in Estonian to tłoka in
Polish, kalaka in Hungarian, and toloka in Ukrainian. Shared work has also
been turned into owed work: Lithuanian estate regulations of 1557 already
list talka among the duties peasants owed the estate. One study of the
Hutsul region records toloka also as family pasture held across
generations near the homes — family land, not common land. Which sense
this valley uses is for the elders [for the elders]. The guide uses the
word for shared work freely given, and never as a name for unpaid labour
an economy depends on.

**Ubuntu.** The Nguni proverb umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu, in Xhosa and Zulu:
a person is a person through other persons. "I am, because we are; and
since we are, therefore I am" is John Mbiti's rendering (African Religions
and Philosophy, 1969), not the proverb's translation. The idea is
contested within African philosophy; Mboti, among others, warns against
reading it as a claim that Africans are naturally harmony-seeking. The
framework this commons works within, UBEC, the Ubuntu Bioregional Economic
Commons DAO, carries the word in its name. We describe our use of it —
that personhood here is read as reaching into the land a community belongs
to — and say that this extension is ours, not the proverb's. We do not
speak for it.

**Social threefolding.** Rudolf Steiner proposed it in memoranda of 1917
and campaigned for it in 1919: freedom in cultural life, equality in the
life of rights, fraternity in economic life. The hamlet's adults use it as
a lens (Section 25). It is held as method, not worldview; never taught to
children; asked of no family. It is contested, and so is its author: the
1917 memoranda cast Western democracy as Anglo-American domination of
central Europe. Communities partly organised on these lines have also
shown where it breaks; Section 25 records the caution.

**Forest school.** The name belongs to an existing movement: settings that
emerged in Denmark in the 1950s, reached the United Kingdom through
Bridgwater College in 1993, and have had a professional body there, the
Forest School Association, since 2012. The commons uses the words
descriptively. It claims no affiliation and no accreditation.

**Hutsul.** The people and the highland whose practice this place stands
inside. Nothing Hutsul in the commons is ours: the form of settlement, the
enclosed homestead (ґражда) and the outlying group of homesteads (кут),
the polonyna and its season, the local sense of toloka, songs, dances, and
the customs of mourning are for the elders to confirm, correct, or
withhold [for the elders]. Where this document or the grounding documents
draw on them, those passages are marked for the elders and wait on their
word; nothing in them is stated as confirmed until the elders have spoken.

**The world's traditions.** The comparative survey behind the philosophy
names traditions from the Baltic to Aotearoa that the practice here learns
from. The commons names a tradition by its people, never practises it as
its own, and never uses it as evidence. That a shape recurs across many
traditions says something about people. It does not show that a method
works.

### 11. What the commons will not do

- Place a child in any form: no name, face, detail, date, or count. The
  programme is described in standing format only.
- Name the site, or publish anything that places it — a plan with
  distances or terrain, a photograph with a face, signage, or a ridgeline,
  data finer than valley resolution — until the decision on naming the
  site is taken.
- Claim above its level, or print a figure that is not measured, quoted,
  or marked.
- Publish what the elders have not consented to, or anything of a resident
  household's life without that household's own Gate 1.
- Teach a doctrine. The convictions are lived in the open, where a family
  can see them before choosing; none is taught as truth, and no family's
  faith is asked to move.
- Speak for a people or a tradition (Sections 8.5 and 10).
- Sell. No module is held back to create demand for help. Help beyond the
  guide is a separate arrangement and does not close the guide.
- Launch. No network, no campaign, no movement. One other site first.
- Build an economy on unpaid residents, or on work that is owed rather
  than given.
- Carry application or funder material. Anything naming an entity goes to
  funders, not to the repository.

### 12. Where the convictions stand against the evidence

- **The method travels.** Tested when a site in another valley builds from
  the modules and returns its figures, which is L4. Today: no such site.
- **The guide stands without its writers.** Tested when a site builds from
  it with no help from the people who wrote it. Today: untested.
- **Land regenerates with the work.** The nursery and planting modules
  would measure seedling survival and returning ground flora across
  seasons. Today: the nursery module designed at L1, planting at L0, no
  season measured.
- **A place teaches, when it is attended to.** Tested when pattern cards
  are confirmed by independent observers across groups and seasons. Today:
  the settlement walk not yet made, the pattern record not yet begun.
- **Care circulates.** The guide measures nothing that would test it and
  claims nothing from it.
- **Peace grows from tended ground.** Nor this. The guide is one piece of
  infrastructure the conviction would predict; if it is replicated, that
  shows the guide works, not that peace grew.

The commons stands on convictions it cannot prove, and on a method it has
designed and documented but not yet seen travel. Every module says the
same of itself, at its own level.

## Part II — Pathway: how the guide is built and how it travels

### 13. What is being built

Two things are being built at once, and confusing them wastes both.

The first is a place: buildings, water, power, sanitation, a forest, a
programme. It exists to serve the children who come and the household that
hosts them, and it would be worth doing if no one ever copied it.

The second is a description of the place, accurate enough that people
fifty or five hundred kilometres away can build their own version without
repeating the expensive mistakes. That description is the guide.

The guide is not a by-product of the first thing. It is its own work, with
its own standard, its own labour, and its own cost — and if it is treated
as something to write up afterwards, it will not be written. What follows
is the pathway to producing it deliberately.

The place is also becoming a settlement. The forest school serves adults
too — retreat and continuing education — and around it a small hamlet is
planned: households living there, with work, learning, and land held
together. The hamlet is part of the place, and its description is part of
the guide: the place's own pattern language, discovered and not imposed,
and the modules that implement it (Part III).

**A model, not a template.** Other initiatives in the Carpathians —
Ukrainian, Romanian, Polish, Slovak, Hungarian — work in different
regulations, currencies, soils, and histories. What travels is the method,
the sizing, the order of decisions, and the record of what failed. What
does not travel is the answer. A guide that pretends otherwise is an
export, and this work is not for export; it is with.

* * *

### 14. The unit: the module

Everything in the guide is a module with the same shape, so that a reader
who has read one can navigate any other, and so that translation and
revision stay tractable. The shape is fixed in the module template, in
standards/.

**Every module carries:**

- **Purpose** — what problem it solves, in two sentences, and one line of
  connections: what the module depends on and what it feeds.
- **Pattern line** — under Connections: what it grows from and feeds among
  the place's own patterns, once their cards exist; which of Alexander's
  patterns it resonates with, as prompts and never as specification; and
  what it is in tension with (Section 27.3).
- **Context of validity** — the conditions under which the design holds:
  climate, altitude, group size, season of use. The most important
  section, and the one usually missing from published projects.
- **Sizing method** — the arithmetic, with the input a reader must measure
  themselves, not our result.
- **Bill of materials** — quantities, with substitutions that are known to
  work.
- **Build** — sequence, trades needed, time, what happens if it is done in
  the wrong order.
- **Cost** — with currency, month, and place of the quotation. A cost
  without a date is a rumour.
- **Operation and maintenance** — daily, seasonal, annual; who is
  responsible.
- **Failure modes** — what breaks, what it looks like when it is going
  wrong, what it costs to fix.
- **What we got wrong** — the section that makes the guide worth reading.
- **Regulatory notes** — by country, as a table, each entry dated and
  attributed to whoever checked it (Section 8.6); and, at the head of
  the module, the note that permits and local rules may apply wherever
  it is built.
- **Adaptation notes** — what a reader must change for a drier valley, a
  colder one, a smaller group; and the seed sentence: the design is a
  seed, not a copy — a different place grows a different form from it.
- **Provenance and licence** — who wrote it, from whose knowledge, under
  what terms, with its Gate 1 and wartime review.
- **What to check** — every figure marked [set] or [assumed], and how it
  will be replaced.

The three technical guides already drafted — water, energy, sanitation —
feed twelve rows of the component map between them, not three. The map's
source column shows which. Converting them is the first task of the
pathway, not a later tidy-up.

* * *

### 15. Maturity: what may be claimed

Every module carries a level, stated at the top. Readers act on the level,
not on the confidence of the prose.

| Level | Meaning | May publish |
|---|---|---|
| L0 | Idea, discussed | nothing, or a marked draft |
| L1 | Designed and costed, basis per line | design, marked untested |
| L2 | Built here | build record, real cost |
| L3 | Measured through a full season | performance numbers |
| L4 | Built by someone else, elsewhere | their figures beside ours |
| L5 | Improved by others, merged back | the improved version |

Whether a cost is quoted, estimated, or assumed is a label on each cost
line, not a level. A module at L1 may be costed entirely from estimates,
and says so line by line.

**A marked draft.** An L0 module that carries a sizing method and a
design may be published as a marked draft: its status says so, its
first lines say so, every figure in it is marked, and it carries Gate 1
and a wartime review like any other release. It claims nothing; a
reader acts on L0. Decided 2026-09-28, so that work in progress can be
read and co-created in the open.

The three existing guides sit at L1. Nothing in them should be read as
tested, and the modules must say so on their face. The temptation the
pathway has to resist is publishing L1 work in L3 language.

A module's maturity level says what is built. A pattern card's status says
what is known about the place. The two are kept apart (Section 27.5).

without sun or fuel and a general circuit that may go dark, the array,
the battery, the inverter, the generator and its fuel store, and the
shelter's own small supply, with the loads every other module puts on
them. Micro-hydro adds a winter source to the same circuits and takes
nothing away but part of the array; the generator stays as a reserve,
and the battery as it is. The wood heat row holds every wood fire on
the site — boiler, buffer tank, and radiators; the water heater and the
solar collectors with their tank; the kitchen's cookstove and the
gathering place's masonry heater as appliances; their chimneys — and
the firewood for all of them. The washroom row takes the hot water from
the tank on; the kitchen and the gathering place rows decide whether
and where a fire stands in their rooms. No heat is made with
electricity; a turbine's surplus may go to the buffer tank through its
dump load. Firewood is a priced running cost; the forest strand yields
none, and wood from the household's own woodland enters only through a
permitted felling by paid hands, shown as material contribution beside
the price.

Where the Carpathian Earthship meets the other rows. The Earthship row
holds one building dug into a slope, with its turf carried on over the
roof: the structure of a protective shelter, the food store's pantry
and root cellar, a winter garden at the entrance with a wellness room
and sauna, and a working room above. The shelter row keeps the
standard, the siting, and the certification, and the food storage row
the keeping and preserving; the wood heat row holds the building's
stoves, flues, and hot water, and the energy row the shelter's fan and
light on the essential circuit. The shelter is described in standing
format there as everywhere: what it must do and how it is sized, never
where it stands, and its own supply without its figures.

### 17. The pathway, in five phases

**Phase 0 — Standards and repository.** Fix the module template, the file
naming, the versioning, the units and currency conventions, the image
rules, and where the repository lives. Convert the three existing guides
to module shape. Nothing is published yet. Weeks, not months, and it is
the phase most often skipped.

**Phase 1 — Document what exists.** Before any new building: measure and
write up what the site already has. The spring's yield. The existing flush
toilets and where they discharge. The buildings, their construction, their
heat loss. Walk the settlement with Alexander's patterns as prompts
(Section 26) and begin the pattern record. This is the baseline every
later figure is read against, and it can only be captured once.

**Phase 2 — Build and measure.** Each module moves L1 to L2 as it is
built, with the build recorded as it happens — dated photographs, real
invoices, the day the mason said the wall needed to be thicker. Then a
full season of measurement before any performance claim: water in the dry
month, battery through December, the reed bed under snow.

**Phase 3 — Publish.** English settles first; the Ukrainian sibling
follows and is corrected over time rather than held back. Publication is
module by module as each reaches L2, not a single release at the end. A
module that is honest about being L1 can be published early and marked.

**Phase 4 — Invite replication.** One or two sites first, not a launch. A
site that is already building something and needs two of our modules is
worth more than fifty readers. Their figures come back into the module
beside ours, which is what L4 means.

**Phase 5 — Commons governance.** Once other people's work is in the
guide, it needs a way of deciding what gets merged, who holds the
repository, and what happens if the original site stops. Deferred
deliberately — do not build governance for a commons that has one member.

* * *

### 18. The first twelve months

A sequence, not a schedule. Each step assumes the one before. Section 3
says where the work stands.

1. Fix the module template, the repository, and the site parameters.
   Convert the three guides into the twelve rows they feed.
2. Baseline the site: spring yield in the dry month, existing sanitation
   and its outfall, buildings and their condition, the stream if there is
   one. Walk the settlement with Alexander's patterns as prompts, and
   begin the pattern record.
3. Send the written enquiries: establishment category, the spring's
   protection zone, discharge to ground, a fire-fighting reserve, a
   water-use permit for the stream. File each answer against the
   regulatory row of the module it governs.
4. Write the safeguarding module first among the programme modules — it is
   at L2 already and it governs the rest.
5. Publish the utility modules at L1, marked as untested, in English and
   Ukrainian.
6. Build the safety and utility layers; record as built, not after.
7. Measure one full season, including one winter.
8. Move the built modules to L2, then L3 where a season supports it.
9. Find one other site. Not a network, not a launch. One.

### 19. Licensing, and what cannot be licensed

**Documents:** CC BY-SA 4.0, as everything else in this project. Share,

**Designs and drawings:** a hardware-appropriate licence rather than a

**Measurement data:** released openly, ideally with no conditions beyond

### 20. How another site would use this

The replication protocol, written from the reader's side:

1. **Self-assessment.** Group size, season of use, altitude, water source,
   grid, legal status. Half a page, which decides which modules apply.
2. **Measure first.** Each module names what must be measured before it
   can be used. A site that skips the spring measurement and builds our
   cistern has not replicated anything.
3. **Take modules, not the model.** A site that needs only sanitation
   takes sanitation. Walk your own settlement with Alexander's patterns as
   prompts, and begin your own pattern record, as in Section 26. Your
   knowledge-holders name your patterns; ours are not yours.
4. **Localise the regulation.** Your row in the table, checked by you,
   sent back.
5. **Build, record, return figures.** Real costs in your currency and
   month; what you changed and why.
6. **Twinning, if wanted.** A working relationship between two households
   beats any amount of documentation, and the documentation exists mostly
   to make the first conversation shorter.

A failure log is part of the offer: sites that tried something from the
guide and abandoned it, with the reason. The absence of such a log in most
published projects is why most published projects cannot be trusted.

**At another site,** your own word replaces присілок; your own
knowledge-holders stand where "the elders" stand here; and your own law
replaces every Ukrainian reference in this document.

### 21. Who pays for the documentation

Documentation time is real work — roughly a day per module to draft, and
more to check, draw, and translate. It is invisible in most budgets, which
is why most projects do not have documentation.

Three routes, not exclusive:

- **Inside the infrastructure budget.** A documentation line of five to
  eight percent of capital, justified as open educational resources, which
  several funders fund willingly.
- **As its own small grant.** Open knowledge, digital commons, and
  educational resource programmes fund exactly this, and the deliverable
  is unusually easy to verify.
- **In kind.** The household's time, counted as contribution at the same
  rate as toloka labour, so its value appears rather than vanishing.

The guide is not sold and is not a product. If someone wants more than the
guide gives — a person on site, a design for their valley — that is a
separate arrangement and does not close the guide.

## Part III — The open hamlet: how the settlement grows and governs itself

### 22. The question

Is the forest school, rightly seen, a hamlet — a small settlement of
households, work, learning, and land — run as a living lab, and published
as an open hamlet the way the modules are published as open educational
resources?

The forest school is not only a place for children. It is also a place for
adults — retreat and continuing education — and around it a small
settlement is planned: households living there, with work, learning, and
land held together. The open hamlet is that settlement, documented as
openly as the modules. It is run as a living lab, and it comes to know
itself through citizen science (Section 9.5).

The answer taken on 2026-09-27 was yes, subject to the checks with the
elders (Section 29), with three consequences for the component map.
Programme gains an adult strand — retreat and continuing education. Legal
form and ownership stays an L0 row and becomes a precondition: nobody
lives on the site before it is settled. Safeguarding must cover retreat
guests and residents, not only visiting adults during sessions: a design
question for the hamlet.

* * *

### 23. What a hamlet is in this landscape

#### 23.1 As the literature holds it

Every point below is for the elders to confirm, correct, or withhold
before it is written down as ours [for the elders].

- **Dispersed settlement.** Separate homesteads scattered on the slopes,
  far from each other, placed near the pastures rather than gathered in
  the valley.
- **Village core and outliers.** A core in the valley — church, school,
  shop — with homesteads standing singly or grouped in outlying parts
  called kut, which belong to the village but lie far from it.
- **The enclosed homestead, ґражда (grazhda).** House and farm buildings
  joined by a high roofed wall into a closed yard with one gate; described
  as the form of the better-off, standing beside the open yard with freely
  placed buildings, and now seen mostly in museums.
- **Homestead, hayfield, forest, polonyna.** A land mostly of forest and
  meadow, with little arable ground; summer pasture on the polonyna, with
  its own seasonal buildings and enclosures, and the herd back down by the
  end of September.
- **толока (toloka).** Used in the programme for shared work; recorded in
  one study of the region also as family pasture held across generations
  near the homes (Section 10).

The literature disagrees on the altitude of traditional dwellings. No
figure enters our text until it is resolved.

#### 23.2 The word for it

| Word | What it carries | Use |
|---|---|---|
| присілок | small settlement belonging to a village | working term |
| кут | Hutsul outlying group of homesteads | for the elders |
| поселення | settlement; since 2024 also a legal term | avoid loosely |
| спільнота | the community of people | for the people |
| громада | also the territorial community | avoid |
| хутір | isolated farmstead | too small |

**Working title in Ukrainian:** відкритий присілок. The elders' word
replaces it if they give one.

**A legal note, unchecked.** Law of Ukraine No. 3285-IX, in force from 26
January 2024, recognises town, selyshche, and village as the categories of
populated place, and is reported to give поселення a narrow legal sense: a
compact place of residence outside any populated place, without a stable
population. Read from secondary sources only; the statute text is to be
read before the word is used. A seasonal retreat and education site may
fall inside that sense, which makes the word a legal question and not only
a translation.

**One term avoided.** Родове помістя and the settlements built around it
belong to a particular movement, and a plain екопоселення carries some of
that association.

### 25. Three spheres, as a lens

The hamlet's adults govern themselves with a lens taken from Rudolf
Steiner's social threefolding (Section 10), used as method and not as
worldview — never taught to children, and asked of no family. Three
questions are put to every decision:

- **Cultural life — whose freedom is this?** Learning, the adult strand,
  the elders' culture, each family's faith, the pattern record: run by
  those who carry them, and funded by gift, never by sale. Where teaching
  is paid, as in retreats, the price is set in the economic sphere and the
  content stays with those who teach.
- **Rights life — what holds for everyone alike?** Standing rules,
  membership, consent, the tenure of the land, and safeguarding above all:
  equal for every person, and not for sale to money or pedagogy.
- **Economic life — what need, met by which association?** Meals, trades,
  shared work, the nursery, the garden, retreat income: agreed among
  those who produce and those who use. The garden's association of
  growers and eaters has two circles — the hamlet first, the region only
  once the hamlet is fed.

**Self-governance, designed threefold.** Those who teach decide pedagogy;
all members decide the standing rules, each with one voice; the
associations of those who produce and use decide the economy. Safeguarding
sits in the rights sphere, beyond the reach of the other two.

The lens is not taken one to one. The theory is contested; the state is
part of the rights sphere, and under martial law more than ever; and the
place may hold its own forms of the same three, or others, for the elders
to name.

Where the same few people sit in all three spheres — as they will in a
hamlet of a few households — the lens still asks which question is being
decided, not who decides it.

**A caution from practice.** Camphill communities, partly organised on
these lines, lived for decades with co-workers who were unpaid and housed
as families. At one of them, from 2014, that model met charity governance
and employment rules, co-workers began to be made employees, and
residents' legal challenge was refused in 2015. No economy here is
designed to rest on unpaid residents without a footing in Ukrainian labour
law and the rules for children's establishments.

**Where the spheres show in Alexander's prompts.**

- **Cultural:** 18 Network of Learning, 43 University as a Marketplace, 83
  Master and Apprentices, 85 Shopfront Schools, 181 The Fire, 185 Sitting
  Circle.
- **Rights:** 36 Degrees of Publicness, 44 Local Town Hall, 53 Main
  Gateways, 61 Small Public Squares, 67 Common Land, 69 Public Outdoor
  Room.
- **Economic:** 9 Scattered Work, 41 Work Community, 46 Market of Many
  Shops, 87 Individually Owned Shops, 91 Traveler's Inn, 148 Small Work
  Groups, 156 Settled Work, 157 Home Workshop, 177 Vegetable Garden, 178
  Compost.
- **Where they meet, and where one most easily swallows another:** 45
  Necklace of Community Projects, 80 Self-Governing Workshops and Offices,
  129 Common Areas at the Heart, 147 Communal Eating.

Patterns named here but not in Section 26 have not been walked; they are
for the reader to look up.

   residual flow, the fish, and the valley's own sense of the stream
   decide whether a turbine is built at all.

### 27. The bridge between patterns and modules

#### 27.1 What is true

Modules are not simply the construction-scale implementation of patterns,
and patterns are not a layer above the component map. The relation is many
to many: one module touches several patterns, and one pattern draws on
several modules. Patterns cut across the map as a second way of reading
the same place — the map says what is built, the patterns say why and
where it belongs.

Evidence flows back. A module measured through a season is citizen science
data for the pattern cards it touches. Patterns give modules their reason;
modules give patterns their evidence.

#### 27.2 Three kinds of pattern

| Kind | Named by | Cited as |
|---|---|---|
| The place's | household, co-creators, elders | card name |
| Alexander's | the book, as a prompt | number and name |
| A module's design rules | us, as designers | the module's text |

A module's own design rules — the water module's four rules — stay inside
the module and are tested by its maturity level, not by pattern status.

#### 27.3 The pattern line

Every module carries, under its Connections line:

- **Grows from** and **Feeds** — the place's patterns, by card name, once
  the card exists; until then, "not yet named";
- **Resonates with** — Alexander's, by number and name, as prompts;
- **In tension with** — any pattern the module pulls against.

Only the place's patterns are grown from and fed. Alexander's are
resonance, never specification. The project's adapted pattern card carries
the return line, **Implemented by**, naming the modules. The Erdpuls card
is not edited.

#### 27.4 The water module as the example

- **Grows from:** not yet named. Two questions for the elders before the
  spring box is built — how homesteads here stand to their spring, and
  whether this spring is held in any way that decides how it may be
  touched. The SPADOK heritage page on the Hutsul dwelling places the
  outbuildings of a yard by relief, road, garden, and distance to the
  spring: a candidate place pattern, the elders' to name.
- **Resonates with:** 207 Good Materials, 83 Master and Apprentices, 104
  Site Repair.
- **In tension with:** 64 Pools and Streams — the buried cistern and piped
  main take water out of sight; the spring and catchment are where it can
  stay visible. 25 Access to Water — the catchment fence keeps people back
  from water the pattern would open to them.

#### 27.5 Status and level

Pattern status — first observation, confirmed, contested, refined,
archived — says what is known about the place. Maturity level says what is
built. They meet in one place only: a module at L3 is a data source for
its patterns' cards. No card is confirmed on a module's evidence alone; it
still needs independent observations across groups and seasons.

### 28. The open hamlet

#### 28.1 What the open hamlet is

Two things published side by side, neither complete without the other:

- **The pattern language of this place** — discovered, not imposed; named
  by the household, the co-creators, and the elders; recorded on cards;
  told once a year as a narrative, as the Pattern Language Assembly Guide
  sets out.
- **The modules** — how the place is built and run, each carrying its
  pattern line.

The hamlet itself is never published as a plan. What travels is the
language and the modules: a seed, not a copy.

**Published, when each is ready:** modules from L1, marked; the pattern
language as a narrative, at valley resolution, after the year's synthesis;
schematic diagrams of relationship between parts of the hamlet. **Not
published:** any site plan with distances, until the decision on naming
the site; pattern cards in full, which carry the most detail of place;
anything of the elders' without their consent, or of a resident
household's life without its own Gate 1 (Section 2).

#### 28.2 The pattern card, adapted

The Erdpuls card, with six rules for this project:

- no child's name; discoveries attributed to the cohort;
- place recorded at ring level only — body, homestead, garden and forest
  edge, village, valley — never by coordinates;
- names in the discoverers' own language first;
- an **Implemented by** line naming the modules;
- an open **Form** field — pattern, rhythm, movement, relation, or another
  the place shows — so the card does not force Alexander's shape on what
  is found;
- an optional **Sphere** field — cultural, rights, economic, or where they
  meet — left empty when none fits.

The Erdpuls card lives in the Pattern Discovery Toolkit, EN v1.3, in the
Erdpuls OER collection. The adapted card is
standards/template_pattern_card_EN_v0_1.md. Pattern files in the workshop
are named pattern_[name]_[LANG]_v[major]_[minor].md.
